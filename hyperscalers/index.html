Hyperscalers — who builds and hosts AI capacity

Chips and software matter only if someone pays for floorspace, power, and clusters. This page maps hyperscale demand owners and capacity builders — integrated cloud operators (US and Chinese), GPU clouds (neoclouds), miner / AI landlords, and colocation / datacenter REIT hosts.

Hub section · five layers Sources: hub extraction + public seed JSON · Claude audit Oct 2026 Not investment advice

Why hyperscalers sit in the middle of the AI stack

Foundries make chips; hardware vendors sell boxes; software vendors train models. Hyperscalers are the large buyers and builders who turn those pieces into multi-gigawatt data centers and cloud services.

  • Own and build capacity. Integrated cloud operators (Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Meta, Oracle, plus Chinese peers) spend tens to hundreds of billions of dollars a year in capital expenditure (CapEx) on land, power, cooling, and AI clusters.
  • Specialize in GPU rental. GPU cloud providers (CoreWeave, Nebius, SpaceXAI / Colossus, Lambda, and peers) run dense accelerator fleets — often leasing space and power from colo landlords while selling training and inference capacity to labs and enterprises.
  • Host the footprint. Colocation providers, datacenter REITs, and former bitcoin-mining landlords own facilities and interconnection hubs; in many deals the hyperscaler is a tenant of the colo, not the building owner.

Who-is-who by layer

1. Integrated cloud operators

Large technology companies that own or build AI data-center capacity at massive CapEx and sell infrastructure as a service, platform tools, and first-party AI products. Framing here is operator / builder, not “tenant only.”

5. Offtake, PPAs & ratepayer protection

Hyperscalers sit on the demand and contracting side of AI power — they buy scarcity under long-term contracts and increasingly face rules that keep residential customers from paying for data-center-driven grid upgrades. Generation, turbines, nuclear fuel, and small modular reactors live under Electrification; this layer is how cloud operators and GPU clouds show up as offtakers.

  • Long-term power purchase agreements. A power purchase agreement (PPA) is a multi-year contract to buy electricity (and sometimes capacity) at agreed terms. Hyperscalers sign PPAs with merchant generators such as Constellation, Vistra, and Talen — locking in megawatts at market (or negotiated) prices when clean or firm power is scarce. Buildout energy badges on this page (nuclear, solar, gas, fusion) are the same idea at a glance.
  • Beneficiary-pays tariffs. More than 25 utilities across 19 U.S. states have filed data-center-specific or “beneficiary-pays” tariffs: the large load that causes the upgrade should cover related network costs, so ordinary residential ratepayers are not stuck with the bill.
  • Ratepayer Protection Pledge. The White House Ratepayer Protection Pledge (March 2026) asks hyperscalers to commit to build, bring, or buy generation and to cover related infrastructure — aligning corporate CapEx with the power system they stress. In July 2026 the pledge expanded to 23 governors and 187 utilities / developers. The U.S. House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act (H.R. 9340) by 417–3 on 2026-09-16.
  • Regional contrast. Outcomes differ by market. In some states (for example Nebraska, New Mexico, North Dakota), bills can fall as new industrial load spreads fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours. In the PJM capacity market, the 2028/29 Base Residual Auction cleared at about $325+ per megawatt-day — a shock that hits all customers in that footprint, not only data-center owners. Optionally noted: the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) chair criticized a delayed PJM backstop auction on 2026-09-30.
  • Utility-scale M&A tied to Data Center Alley load. NextEra Energy (NEE) and Dominion Energy (D) announced a pending all-stock acquisition / merger valued around $66.8–67B — not a data-center “package” or partnership. It faces Virginia opposition and guided closing in the second half of 2027. The deal sits next to Data Center Alley load (~51 GW class) and generation optionality under regulation — another path where hyperscaler demand shapes who builds what, and who pays.
