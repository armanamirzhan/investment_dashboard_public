Co-packaged optics (CPO) & optical I/O

Why AI factories hit data-movement walls, when copper loses to light, how today’s GPU networking stack works, and how co-packaged optics (CPO) and on-package optical I/O change who sits in the middle of that hop.

Parent · Photonics Hardware · AI computation Not investment advice

1. Interconnect bottlenecks — why AI factories hit data-movement walls

An “AI factory” packs thousands of accelerators into racks and rows so they can train and serve large models together. The chips got faster; moving bits between them is the hard part.

Inside a hall, GPUs must exchange gradients, activations, and expert tokens at enormous rates. When the wires cannot keep up, GPUs sit idle waiting on data — wasted capital and power. After high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and advanced packaging ease, many industry maps expect optical interconnects to become the next dominant pinch around ~2027–2028 (see the parent Photonics scarcity brief).

Two different jobs get confused in headlines:

  • Scale-out — linking racks and clusters through network switches (Ethernet / InfiniBand fabrics).
  • Scale-up — linking GPUs inside a tightly coupled domain (today often copper NVLink-class meshes; tomorrow, more optical I/O on the package).

CPO shows up in both stories, but the endgames differ. Scale-out can still use a switch ASIC with optics packaged beside it. Pure scale-up optical I/O aims to remove a separate switch chip from that hop.

2. Direct copper vs photonics

Copper wins on short hops. Light wins when distance, power, or crosstalk make thick cables painful. The crossover distance is collapsing as lane rates climb.

Rule of thumb for AI halls today:

  • Under about one meter — direct copper (board traces, direct-attach copper (DAC) cables) usually wins on cost, latency, and simplicity.
  • Beyond a few meters — optics dominate; even mid-reach links often use active optical cables (AOC) or pluggable optical modules.
  • At 800 gigabits per second and 1.6 terabits per second class links — the useful copper reach is collapsing toward roughly 1–2 meters. Shielding, equalization, and retimers get expensive; heat and latency add up.
What each side “pays”

Copper pays in shielding, channel equalization, retimers, and latency as rates rise. Optics pay an electron↔photon conversion cost (lasers, modulators, detectors, and often a digital signal processor (DSP) inside the module). CPO and on-package optical I/O try to shrink that conversion tax by moving optics next to the compute or switch die and sharing external light sources (ELS).

3. Today’s stack — GPU → SerDes → networking ASIC → optical module

Most AI clusters still move data the “classic” way: the GPU talks electrically, a networking chip switches packets, and a pluggable optical module turns those bits into light at the faceplate.

  1. GPU / accelerator — e.g. NVIDIA (NVDA), AMD (AMD), or a hyperscaler custom chip. High-speed serializer/deserializer (SerDes) blocks push bits off the die.
  2. Scale-up fabric (often copper) — NVIDIA’s NVLink mesh ties GPUs inside a node or rack domain. The open UALink effort aims at a multi-vendor accelerator fabric.
  3. Networking ASIC — Ethernet or InfiniBand switch silicon from Broadcom (AVGO), NVIDIA (NVDA) (Spectrum / Quantum lines), Marvell (MRVL), and peers.
  4. Front-panel optics — DAC / AOC for short reaches, or pluggable transceivers (QSFP / OSFP cages) that convert electrical lanes to fiber. Module assemblers such as Fabrinet (FN) and platform vendors such as Cisco (CSCO) and Arista (ANET) sit in this faceplate world.

Retimers and connectivity silicon from names like Credo (CRDO) and Astera Labs (ALAB) help copper channels survive higher lane rates between chips, boards, and cages.

Why there is no simple “blade sandwich” of optics today

People sometimes imagine sliding optical engines between every GPU like a mezzanine card. In practice that hits limits:

  • Mezzanine lane rates — many board-to-board connectors top out around ~112 gigabits per second per lane class, while switch and GPU roadmaps push toward 224G-class signaling.
  • Crosstalk and signal integrity — packing dense electrical lanes beside hot, noisy compute boards is hard.
  • Serviceability — pluggable faceplate modules are easy to swap; burying optics under a GPU stack is not, unless the light source is designed to be external and replaceable (ELS / ELSFP).

4. Near future — CPO beside the switch or compute die

The next step puts the optical engine next to the big chip on the same package or substrate, instead of only at the front panel. Lasers often stay off-package as an external light source (ELS) / ELSFP module so they can be swapped and cooled separately.

5. Endgame — GPU SerDes drives the modulator; external laser; no separate switch ASIC on that hop

On the pure optical-I/O scale-up path, the long-run picture looks like this: the GPU’s own SerDes drives a silicon-photonic modulator; an external indium phosphide (InP) laser (or multi-wavelength ELS) feeds light; fiber carries the hop; there is no separate switch ASIC in the middle of that particular link.

Nuance — do not over-read “who gets sidelined”

Scale-out networking can still use CPO switch ASICs (Broadcom Bailly, NVIDIA Spectrum-X / Quantum-X Photonics, and peers). The “sidelined on that hop” framing applies to the tight GPU-to-GPU optical-I/O scale-up path, not to the entire networking industry. Pluggables, retimers, and faceplate optics also remain relevant wherever copper reach, serviceability, or brownfield racks still win.

Who tends to stay relevant on that optical-I/O path

  • Lumentum (LITE) — lasers / optical engines / ELS supply.
  • Coherent (COHR) — lasers and broader photonics.
  • AXT (AXTI) — upstream InP / compound substrates for many lasers.
  • TSMC (TSM) — COUPE and advanced packaging for optics beside compute.
  • POET Technologies (POET) — optical interposer / engine approaches aimed at lower-cost light engines.
  • Private optical-I/O specialists (Ayar Labs, Lightmatter) if they win sockets — no tickers.

Who can be sidelined on that pure scale-up hop

  • Broadcom (AVGO) / Marvell (MRVL) switch ASICs for that hop — less needed if GPUs talk optically peer-to-peer (they remain central for scale-out CPO switches).
  • Credo (CRDO) / Astera Labs (ALAB) retimers on that hop — less copper channel to rescue (retimers still matter elsewhere in the rack).
  • Fabrinet (FN) pluggable module assembly for that hop — engines move on-package (Fabrinet can still build other optics).
  • Cisco (CSCO) / Arista (ANET) faceplate optics role on that hop — less front-panel conversion inside a pure optical scale-up domain (they remain core to Ethernet switching and campus/DC networking).
