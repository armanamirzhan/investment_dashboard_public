Generation: turbines, engines, fuel cells and nuclear — who makes the electricity for AI, and what is scarce

The second of three power subsections. This page covers the equipment that makes the electricity, from gas turbines and the castings inside them to reactors and the uranium, enrichment and forging chain behind them. Written by Claude after two research reports: thermal and onsite generation (G1) and nuclear, small modular reactors and the fuel cycle (G2).

1. The short version

Three facts organize this page. First, the equipment that can deliver power to a data center before 2029 is not the equipment the market associates with the AI power story. The famous names, heavy gas turbines and nuclear reactors, are sold out or unbuilt: the four Western, Japanese and Korean turbine makers hold an order book of roughly 230 gigawatts (GW) against about 54 GW of combined annual output, so a turbine ordered today arrives in 2029–31 [G1-1] [G1-7] [G1-8], and the first new small modular reactor in the United States will not produce power before 2030–32 [G2-40] [G2-56]. What is actually being installed in 2026–28 is reciprocating gas engines (large piston engines running on natural gas), small and refurbished turbines, and fuel cells. The largest engine plants ever announced came in the past twelve months: 2.3 GW for Oracle from VoltaGrid (announced October 2025) built from 92 blocks of 25 megawatts (MW) of INNIO engines, and more than 500 INNIO engines for OpenAI's Shackelford County site in Texas [G1-28] [G1-54]. The engine makers are priced as if this were a stopgap.

Second, the binding constraints sit one or two layers below the famous names. For turbines it is the casting of single-crystal nickel-alloy blades, a step done by a handful of foundries (Howmet, Precision Castparts, Doncasters and the turbine makers' own shops; G1 report, section 6), only one of them listed as a pure exposure (Howmet) [G1-43]. For nuclear it is high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU, the fuel most advanced reactors need and which one US company makes), heavy forgings (three forges in the world can make a large reactor vessel, about four a year each) and reactor coolant pumps (12–16 a year from one plant) [G2-74] [G2-79]. None of these owners is valued for that position.

Third, the market has already repriced the whole group, but unevenly. GE Vernova and Bloom Energy still trade at about 40 and 58 times 2027 earnings, and the reactor developers have fallen 60–86% from their highs without yet earning anything [G2-80]. In between sit companies whose order books rose while their shares fell: INNIO is 51% below its high at about 25 times 2027 earnings after its equipment backlog grew 279%; Mitsubishi Heavy is 27% below its high with a 35 GW turbine book that it fills selectively; BWX Technologies is 43% below its high with a backlog up 40%; Centrus, the only US-owned enricher, is 68% below its high with $4.5 billion of orders [G1-26] [G1-8] [G2-78] [G2-75]. Those gaps are where my picks come from.

Two things are deliberately not on this page. The companies that sell electricity (utilities, merchant generators and the nuclear plant owners Constellation, Vistra and Talen) are on the energy-provider page, because their economics are set by power markets and regulators rather than by factories. The grid-connection equipment (transformers, switchgear and everything inside the data hall) is on the electrification page.

The decisive input is not any of these costs. SemiAnalysis argues that a gigawatt of AI inference can generate on the order of $100 billion of revenue a year, so that paying twice as much for electricity, or accepting 30% lower efficiency, in exchange for faster deployment is "a no-brainer" [G1-54]. As long as that holds, buyers pay for the earliest megawatt and the cost ranking is secondary. If it stops holding, because GPU utilization or token prices fall, the ranking reasserts itself and the aeroderivative and fuel-cell fleets are the first stranded assets. That asymmetry is why I prefer suppliers whose equipment is redeployable (engines) or needed under every scenario (castings, forgings, fuel) over single-customer onsite fleets.

The same gap exists on the demand side. SemiAnalysis tracks about 75 GW of firm, binding orders for behind-the-meter equipment (generation on the data center's own site rather than from the grid), with 20 GW booked in the June 2026 quarter alone, against about 3 GW expected to be running in islanded mode by the end of 2026; Cleanview counts about 90 GW announced and about 2 GW operating [G1-54] [G1-56]. The distance between 75–90 GW ordered or announced and 3 GW operating is where the money will be spent in 2027–29, and where any disappointment would first appear.

4. Eight engineering realities the market has not fully priced

4.1 Engineers have already moved the 2026–28 build to reciprocating engines; the market still calls them a stopgap

The largest onsite plants announced in 2026 are engine plants, not turbine plants: Oracle's 2.3 GW from VoltaGrid uses 92 containerized blocks of 25 MW built on INNIO Jenbacher engines, OpenAI's Shackelford County site in Texas is specified with more than 500 Jenbacher J624 engines for 1.4 GW of computing load, and Wärtsilä booked a 790 MW plant of 42 medium-speed engines in Texas on its way to 2.4 GW of US data-center orders [G1-28] [G1-54] [G1-32]. Engines deliver in 12–24 months, come in small blocks that keep each site under "minor source" emission thresholds, and provide the many spinning generators that an islanded plant needs to ride through a data center's power swings [G1-54]. The order books show the shift: INNIO's equipment backlog rose 279% to $6.6 billion, Caterpillar's large-engine backlog has more than tripled since January 2024 and it is restarting a 10 MW engine line with 1.5 GW of annual capacity from the fourth quarter of 2026, and Wärtsilä plans to raise capacity 2.2 times by 2029 [G1-26] [G1-17] [G1-16] [G1-31]. Yet INNIO trades 51% below its high and 26% below its June IPO price, and Wärtsilä carries a consensus "hold" [X-23].

4.2 Reservations are not orders, and the pricing has not reached revenue yet

GE Vernova expects its 2026 orders to be priced "10 to 20 points higher" per kilowatt than its late-2025 orders, and trade press reports a path to about $600 per kW by the end of 2027, nearly three times the 2019 level [G1-3] [G1-4]. Those prices belong to slots that deliver in 2029–31, so they reach revenue only in 2028–29. Meanwhile 90% of GE Vernova's new signings are reservations [G1-1]. A turbine maker's shares therefore carry two layers of expectation: that the reservations convert, and that the 2028 margins (GE Vernova targets 20% by 2028) hold through a capacity doubling [G1-4]. That is a lot to pay 40 times 2027 earnings for.

4.3 Casting, not assembly, sets the ceiling

The first-stage blades and vanes of a gas turbine run above the melting point of their own alloy and survive through internal cooling channels and ceramic coatings. They are cast in nickel superalloys, often as single crystals, by a handful of foundries: Howmet, Precision Castparts (inside Berkshire Hathaway), Doncasters (private) and the captive foundries of the turbine makers (G1 report, section 6). Elon Musk said "the limiting factor for gas turbine production is casting the blades", and SpaceX bought about 336 hectares at Bastrop, Texas for its own blade foundry; Korean analysts expect no volume production there before 2030 because single-crystal casting "demands a higher level of technical expertise" [G1-46] [G1-12]. Howmet's gas-turbine revenue grew 38% year on year in the June quarter, with customers "already revisiting and adding to their demand outlooks" and capital spending rising again in 2027 [G1-43]. It is the only listed pure exposure to the layer that every turbine expansion must pass through, and aerospace still sets its multiple.

4.4 Permits, not equipment, now pace turbine-based onsite power, which is why one fuel-cell maker owns a niche

The federal rules loosened in 2026: the Environmental Protection Agency's January turbine rule exempts low-use turbines from the heaviest permitting and streamlines temporary installations, and July guidance exempts islanded plants from the acid-rain program [G1-62] [G1-63]. The courts tightened: xAI ran 27 unpermitted mobile turbines at Southaven, Mississippi for seven months before a 41-turbine permit was granted in March 2026, and environmental groups filed notice to sue over permit-splitting at San Antonio data centers in July [G1-61] [G1-63]. In counties that already breach air-quality standards, a solid-oxide fuel cell, which converts gas to electricity without combustion, needs no combustion permit, which is why Oracle's Project Jupiter dropped a turbine design mid-permitting and why Bloom Energy has booked about 3.8 GW against 0.03 GW for FuelCell Energy, the other US solid-oxide maker [G1-54] [G1-38]. The investment problem is the price: Bloom trades at about 58 times 2027 earnings and above the analysts' mean target, one customer was about 73% of its June-quarter revenue, and a short seller questioned its scandium supply in July [G1-37] [G1-39]. Right company, wrong multiple.

4.5 Bridge power is becoming permanent, and the grid's arrival in 2029–31 will re-role the plants

Microsoft's 2 GW campus at Pecos, Texas, with 2.67 GW of gas generation, will run "behind the meter initially" on GE Vernova turbines with a 20-year gas supply from Chevron, with first power in 2028; Solaris Energy Infrastructure has about 2.3 GW of turbines under long-term power contracts; xAI bought the mobile-turbine lessor APR Energy for about $1 billion [G1-60] [G1-25] [G1-24]. Grid connections for the 2025–27 onsite cohort are routinely expected in 2029–31, a median of more than five years from request to operation [G1-59] [G1-55]. When the grid arrives, an onsite plant becomes a peaking or backup asset. Engines and aeroderivative turbines can be moved to the next site; a frame turbine delivered in 2029 and sized for islanded operation cannot. That favors the engine makers and the owners of mobile fleets over the frame-turbine order book, and it is a reason to read the "BTM utility" business models (Solaris, Williams, Kodiak) as contracts with a tenor rather than as permanent utilities.

4.6 Nuclear before 2030 is three restarts and some uprates; everything else is re-labelled existing output

About 9.2 GW of US nuclear output is now under long-term contracts with hyperscalers: Clinton to Meta from June 2027, Susquehanna to Amazon ramping to 2032, Comanche Peak from late 2027, Vistra's four PJM reactors to Meta, and Constellation's 920 MW of new deals [G2-10] [G2-21] [G2-19] [G2-20] [G2-5]. Almost all of it is electricity that already exists. The net-new supply before the end of 2030 is the three restarts, about 2.25 GW (Crane 835 MW in the second half of 2027; Palisades 800 MW, whose fuel loading was paused on 25 September 2026 after a fuel assembly tilted in the vessel, against a March 2027 supply deadline; Duane Arnold 615 MW in the first quarter of 2029 with a $1.9 billion Department of Energy loan), plus about 0.4–0.6 GW of approved uprates [G2-7] [G2-13] [G2-16]. My estimate from the G2 report is that net-new nuclear covers about 4–8% of the increase in US data-center consumption between 2023 and 2030. The clean-firm premium is real but modest: analysts put the Microsoft–Crane contract at $98–115 per megawatt-hour and the Meta–Clinton contract at about $70, roughly $20 above Illinois market revenue, and Constellation frames its uplift as a "$20 to $50" sensitivity [G2-11] [G2-7]. No data-center buyer has yet signed a contract that would pay for a merchant-financed new reactor: at Vogtle's cost an AP1000 needs about $170 per megawatt-hour at an 8% cost of capital, or about $105 with government-rate debt, which is exactly why the June 2026 package of $17.5 billion in Department of Energy loans targets the long-lead components for up to ten AP1000s, and why there are seven letters of intent and zero binding orders [G2-28] [G2-25].

4.7 The nuclear choke points are fuel, forgings and pumps, not reactor designs

Centrus Energy runs the only US-owned HALEU cascade; its $900 million Department of Energy contract (finalized 3 July 2026) keeps the 16-centrifuge cascade running while new capacity arrives "by 2029", and Urenco's HALEU plant in Britain is targeted for 2031 [G2-74] [G2-76]. The Russian low-enriched-uranium import ban ends all waivers on 1 January 2028, and Centrus holds waivers only through 2027 deliveries [G2-64] [G2-65]. Only three forges in the world make Generation III+ reactor pressure vessels, Doosan Enerbility (17,000-tonne press), Japan Steel Works (14,000 tonnes) and China First Heavy Industries, each yielding on the order of four vessels a year; Curtiss-Wright's Cheswick plant makes 12–16 reactor coolant pumps a year, three to four reactors' worth, and committed $80 million in July 2026 to expand [G2-79]. The Department of Energy's $17.5 billion is explicitly aimed at buying these slots early [G2-28]. Meanwhile the listed reactor developers have been repriced violently (Oklo 79% below its high, NuScale 85%, NANO Nuclear 72%, X-energy 60% from its post-IPO high, Terrestrial 86%), Holtec pulled its IPO the day before pricing, and the "order books" they cite are letters of intent [G2-80] [G2-15] [G2-43] [G2-49]. The Department of Energy's reactor pilot program did deliver five criticalities by August 2026, including Oklo's Groves unit, but all five are zero- or low-power test reactors; Groves is a 15-megawatt-thermal isotope test reactor, not Oklo's 75 MW Aurora, whose commercial operation is now targeted for 2028 [G2-46] [G2-57] [G2-47].

4.8 The overbuild is dated: 2030–31

Announced turbine capacity rises from about 54 GW a year today to roughly 80–85 GW a year by 2028–30 (GE Vernova 20 → 24 → 30 GW; Siemens Energy's large-unit lines from 35 to 50 units by 2027; Mitsubishi doubling versus 2024; Doosan from 8 to 12 units a year), against a pre-boom global market of about 30–40 GW a year (my estimate from the G1 report) [G1-1] [G1-7] [G1-9] [G1-11]. The order book covers 2029–30; after that, intake must stay above about 60 GW a year or margins meet a cliff in 2030–31. The first warning would be reservation cancellations, which are not yet disclosed. Mitsubishi's "selective" utility-weighted book is the most defensive of the four; whoever holds the most unconverted reservations in 2029 is the most exposed [G1-8]. Uranium has the same shape, a few years later: the term price is at a record $96.50 per pound while miners have de-rated 16–55% and utilities are "reluctant to contract"; the slow supply response (Kazatomprom's acid-constrained volumes, new mines such as NexGen's Rook I) points to a 2028–35 tightness rather than a 2026–27 one [G2-68] [G2-69] [G2-71].

Glossary

Short explanations of the technical and financial terms used on this page.

Hyperscaler
One of the largest cloud and internet companies (Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta, Oracle and their peers), which build data centers measured in gigawatts and sign the largest power contracts.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization: a measure of operating cash profit used to compare companies with different debt and asset ages.
PJM
The grid operator for 13 eastern US states and Washington, DC, and the market where most US data-center load growth and capacity-price pressure sits.
Equity-accounted stake
A holding (such as Cameco's 49% of Westinghouse) that is not consolidated line by line; only the owner's share of the investee's profit appears in its earnings, so revenue and multiples understate the asset.
IPO and lock-up
An initial public offering is a company's first sale of shares to the public. The lock-up is the period (usually 90–180 days) during which the pre-IPO owners may not sell; when it ends, extra shares can come to market.
Generation III+ reactor
The current generation of large light-water reactors with passive safety systems, such as Westinghouse's AP1000 and Korea's APR1400; they need the largest forgings.
Tenge
Kazakhstan's currency (KZT). Kazatomprom reports in tenge, so its consensus earnings are converted to US dollars for the London line.
Token
The unit of text that an AI model reads or produces; the price per token is what AI companies charge, and it sets how much they can afford to pay for electricity.
MW, GW, MWh
A megawatt (MW) is a million watts of power; a gigawatt (GW) is 1,000 MW, roughly a large power plant. A megawatt-hour (MWh) is one megawatt sustained for an hour; electricity prices are quoted per MWh.
Heavy-duty (frame) gas turbine
The 300–600 MW machines used in utility plants, built by GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi Heavy and Doosan Enerbility. Lead times are now three years or more.
Aeroderivative turbine
A jet engine adapted to drive a generator, typically 25–115 MW. Smaller, faster to install and movable, but less efficient than a frame turbine.
Simple cycle, combined cycle
In simple cycle the turbine's hot exhaust is wasted. In combined cycle the exhaust raises steam in a heat-recovery steam generator to drive a second turbine, lifting efficiency from about 40% to above 60%.
Reciprocating gas engine
A large piston engine, 0.5–20 MW each, that burns natural gas to drive a generator. Engines can be delivered in 12–24 months and are built in modules; makers include INNIO, Caterpillar, Wärtsilä and Cummins.
Slot reservation
A deposit that holds a place in a manufacturer's production queue for a future turbine. It is not yet a firm order and can be cancelled.
Behind the meter, islanded
Behind-the-meter generation sits on the customer's side of the utility meter. An islanded plant has no grid connection at all and must handle every power swing itself.
Prime power, bridge power
Prime power is a site's main supply. Bridge power is temporary supply until a grid connection arrives, which for the 2025–27 projects is expected in 2029–31.
Hot section, single-crystal casting, superalloy
The hot section is the combustor and first turbine stages, which run above the melting point of their alloy. The blades are investment-cast from nickel superalloys, often as a single crystal with no grain boundaries, a process few foundries master.
Minor source, Title V, non-attainment area
US air-permit terms. A minor source stays below emission thresholds and gets a simpler permit; a major source needs a full federal (Title V) operating permit. A non-attainment area is a county that breaches air-quality standards, where new combustion is hardest to permit.
Solid-oxide fuel cell
A device that converts natural gas to electricity through a ceramic electrolyte at high temperature, without combustion, so it needs no combustion permit. The stack is replaced every several years.
Load factor, capacity factor
The share of the time, or of maximum output, that a plant actually produces. Nuclear plants run at about 92%; onsite gas plants for data centers aim for 85% or more.
Levelized cost
The average cost per MWh over a plant's life, combining capital, fuel and operations. Figures on this page are estimates from stated inputs, not published values.
PPA
A power purchase agreement: a long-term contract to buy electricity at an agreed price, for example Microsoft's 20-year contract for the Crane reactor.
Restart, uprate
A restart brings a closed reactor back into service. An uprate raises an existing reactor's licensed output, usually by tens of megawatts, after equipment and analysis upgrades.
AP1000, EPR2, APR1000
Large reactor designs: Westinghouse's AP1000 (1,117 MW; built at Vogtle), EDF's EPR2 (France) and KHNP's APR1000 (Korea).
Small modular reactor (SMR)
A reactor of roughly 75–345 MW designed for factory fabrication. Designs include GE Vernova's BWRX-300, NuScale's US460, TerraPower's Natrium, X-energy's Xe-100 and Oklo's Aurora. The first US construction permit for a light-water SMR (TVA's Clinch River BWRX-300) was issued in September 2026; TerraPower's Natrium, an advanced sodium-cooled design, received its permit in March 2026.
Criticality
The point at which a reactor sustains a chain reaction. Five test reactors reached it in 2026 under a Department of Energy pilot program; it is a technical milestone, not commercial power.
NRC, DOE, construction permit, combined licence
The Nuclear Regulatory Commission licenses reactors; the Department of Energy funds and promotes them. A construction permit allows building; an operating or combined licence allows operation.
Uranium, conversion, enrichment, SWU
Mined uranium (yellowcake) is converted to uranium hexafluoride gas, then enriched to raise the share of the fissile isotope. A separative work unit (SWU) is the standard measure of enrichment effort.
HALEU, LEU
Low-enriched uranium (LEU, up to 5% uranium-235) fuels today's reactors. High-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU, 5–20%) is needed by most advanced designs; Centrus runs the only US-owned HALEU cascade.
TRISO fuel
Ceramic-coated fuel particles that withstand very high temperatures, used in gas-cooled and some molten-salt reactors. BWX Technologies and X-energy's TRISO-X make it.
Reactor pressure vessel, forging
The thick steel vessel that contains the reactor core, made from a single giant forging on a 14,000–17,000-tonne press. Three forges in the world can make the largest ones.
Reactor coolant pump
The pump that circulates water through a pressurized-water reactor. Curtiss-Wright's Cheswick plant is the sole AP1000 supplier, making 12–16 a year.
Term price, spot price (uranium)
The spot price is for immediate delivery; the term price is for multi-year contracts and is what producers actually sell at. The term indicator reached a record $96.50 per pound on 30 September 2026.
In-situ recovery
A uranium mining method that pumps a solution through the ore body underground and recovers the dissolved uranium, used in Kazakhstan and the US. It needs large amounts of sulphuric acid.
EPC, boilermaker
Engineering, procurement and construction: the contractor that builds a plant. Boilermakers are the skilled welders who assemble boilers and heat-recovery steam generators; the US has about 10,200 of them.
Book-to-bill, backlog
Orders received divided by revenue in the same period; above 1 means the backlog is growing. The backlog is the value of firm orders not yet delivered.
GAAP, non-GAAP
GAAP are the standard US accounting rules. Non-GAAP figures are company-adjusted, usually excluding items such as stock compensation or one-off charges.
ADR, GDR, OTC line
Depositary receipts are certificates traded in New York (ADR) or London (GDR) that represent shares of a foreign company. Over-the-counter (OTC) lines trade outside the main exchanges and can be thinly traded.
EPS, forward P/E
Earnings per share. The forward price-to-earnings ratio (Fwd P/E) divides the share price by next fiscal year's consensus earnings per share.
2027E, FY3/28E, FY1/28E
2027E is the estimate for calendar 2027. FY3/28E is the estimate for the fiscal year that ends in March 2028 (the usual Japanese fiscal year), and FY1/28E for the year that ends in January 2028.
Consensus, mean score, LSEG
The average of the analysts who cover a stock: their ratings scored from 1 (strong buy) to 5 (sell), and their 12-month price targets. LSEG (the London Stock Exchange Group) and S&P Global supply the underlying estimates.
n/m
Not meaningful, shown where earnings are negative.
