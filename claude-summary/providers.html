Energy providers: who sells the electricity to AI data centers, and who keeps the profit

The third of three power subsections. This page covers the companies that sell electricity and the fuel behind it: regulated utilities, merchant generators and nuclear plant owners, gas pipelines and compression, and the non-US providers. Written by Claude after two research reports: US utilities and power markets (U1) and the gas chain, renewables and international providers (U2).

1. The short version

The companies that sell electricity to AI data centers split into three groups with different economics, and the market has treated all three the same way in 2026: it sold them. The merchant generators and nuclear owners (Constellation, Vistra, Talen, NRG) raised or reaffirmed their guidance, signed 15-to-20-year contracts with hyperscalers and cleared record capacity prices, yet trade 28–48% below their highs at 8–19 times 2027 earnings [U1-37] [U1-42] [U1-47] [U1-48]. The regulated utilities with large tariff-backed data-center load (PPL, Sempra, FirstEnergy, Xcel, Southern) grow earnings at about 7–9% a year on guidance or consensus and sit near 52-week lows at 14–17 times 2027 earnings with 3–4% dividend yields [U1-70] [U1-64] [U1-66] [U1-63]. The gas pipeline and compression companies, whose backlogs are the cleanest evidence that the demand is real, are between 6% and 30% below their highs [U2-1] [U2-9] [X-23].

The reasons for the sell-off are documented and mostly about rules rather than demand. PJM, the grid operator for 13 eastern states and Washington, DC, now sells capacity at an administered price: three auctions in a row cleared at the cap (about $325–333 per megawatt-day), the cap was extended through the 2029/30 auction, and on 29 September 2026 the federal regulator suspended PJM's new 15-year "backstop" auction into February 2027 [U1-21] [U1-24] [U1-28]. Texas approved a fast connection process but ruled that a data center co-located with a power plant can still be fully curtailed in an emergency [U1-10] [U1-11]. Governors and regulators in Virginia, Georgia and Pennsylvania are pushing back on data-center-driven bills [U1-16] [U1-35]. Meanwhile the interconnection queues that frightened investors in the other direction have been exposed as fiction: Texas has 438 gigawatts (GW) of large-load requests, PJM cut 60 GW of utility forecasts to 34 GW before publishing them, and Exelon's "high-probability" load fell 40% once customers had to post collateral [U1-10] [U1-8] [U1-19].

What survives both corrections is contracted load with collateral, which the U1 report estimates at roughly 100–120 GW nationally, under take-or-pay tariffs now approved in 25 states [U1-15]. That is the number this page is built on. My picks are the generators whose 2027 is already hedged and whose 2028 capacity revenue is already fixed (Vistra, Talen), the owner of the only multi-gigawatt, already-interconnected clean supply a hyperscaler can contract before 2028 (Constellation), the wires utilities with tariff-backed load in the zones that carry the growth (PPL, Sempra's Oncor, PSEG), and the pipe and compression owners that carry the fuel (Kinder Morgan, Kodiak). The equipment that makes the electricity is on the generation page; the grid-connection and in-hall equipment is on the electrification page.

2. Who gets paid for what: three business models and a fuel chain

A regulated utility earns an allowed return (about 9.6–9.8% in recent decisions, with utilities requesting 10.4% or more) on the capital it invests in wires and plants, so data-center load helps it only if regulators let it build and recover the cost; the risk is politics, not price [U1-16]. A merchant generator sells energy and capacity into wholesale markets, so it is a claim on power prices, hedged a few years out; the risk is a price cap or a collapse in the forward curve. A pipeline or compression company sells transport or horsepower under long-term, mostly take-or-pay contracts, so its earnings follow the volume of gas burned rather than the price of either gas or power. The chart shows why the merchant generators in the mid-Atlantic re-rated in 2024–25 and why they stalled in 2026. The capacity price, which every generator receives per megawatt of availability per day, went from $28.92 for the 2024/25 delivery year to $269.92 for 2025/26, then cleared at the newly imposed cap in the three auctions since: $329.17 for 2026/27, $333.44 for 2027/28 and $325.00 for 2028/29 [X-22] [X-21] [U1-21] [U1-23]. The latest auction procured 138,318 MW, 6,831 MW short of the reliability requirement, with only 525 MW of new generation clearing and a total bill of $16.4 billion, of which the market monitor attributes $6.3 billion to data centers [U1-21] [U1-29]. At $325 per MW-day a gigawatt earns about $119 million a year: Talen's 10 GW cleared for 2028/29 is about $1.2 billion of fixed revenue, more than half of its 2026 EBITDA guidance, and PSEG's 3.6 GW of nuclear about $430 million (my estimates) [U1-47] [U1-51].

The cap is now the forecast. Pennsylvania's governor extended it through the 2029/30 auction under a settlement, claiming $45 billion of savings; the first uncapped auction, under a reformed design, is scheduled for May 2027 [U1-24]. The one new avenue to uncapped, long-dated revenue was PJM's Reliability Backstop Procurement, a one-time auction for 15-year contracts at up to $555 per MW-day filed in July 2026; on 29 September 2026 the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission accepted the framework but suspended it for five months over cost allocation and collateral rules, so the bid window never opened [U1-27] [U1-28]. That slip is one documented reason for the September weakness in Constellation, Vistra, Talen and PSEG. The curtail-first rule for new large loads without contracted supply still takes effect on 1 June 2027 [U1-28].

4. Nine realities the market has not fully priced

4.1 The queues are fiction; the contracts with collateral are real

The North American Electric Reliability Corporation raised its ten-year peak-demand growth forecast to 224 GW (+24%), most of it data centers, and Grid Strategies' five-year forecast is 166 GW, of which it says about 25 GW is overstated [U1-2] [U1-3]. The queues are an order of magnitude larger than anything plausible: 438 GW of large-load requests in Texas, 200 GW at Oncor alone, and 60 GW of utility-submitted 2030 load in PJM that the operator cut to 34 GW before publishing [U1-10] [U1-65] [U1-8]. The useful number is what survives a collateral demand. Exelon's "high-probability" load fell 40% to 11 GW when it required transmission security agreements; Dominion reports 12.0 GW of firm agreements out of a 53.8 GW "contracted" pipeline; FirstEnergy 6.4 GW under contract against 24.8 GW; Southern 17 GW under 15-year minimum contracts [U1-19] [U1-52] [U1-66] [U1-56]. Twenty-five states now have approved large-load tariffs: AEP Ohio requires an 85% minimum bill for up to 12 years; Virginia's tariff from January 2027 requires 14-year contracts, payment for 85% of wires and 60% of generation costs regardless of use, and $1.5 million of collateral per MW [U1-15] [U1-13] [U1-16] [U1-17]. These tariffs convert speculative load into a financeable contract and move the stranded-asset risk from ratepayers to hyperscalers' balance sheets, which is what makes regulated rate base defensible.

4.2 The merchants are hedged through 2027, so the de-rating is about 2028 and about rules

Vistra is about 94% hedged for 2027 and 72% for 2028, with a 2027 EBITDA "opportunity" of $7.4–7.8 billion before about $700 million from Cogentrix; Talen is about 70% hedged for 2027 and 30% for 2028 [U1-42] [U1-43] [U1-47]. Constellation raised 2026 guidance to $11.50–12.50 per share and signed 920 MW of new 15-to-20-year nuclear contracts in one quarter, yet closed 36% below its high [U1-37] [U1-38]. The drivers I can document are the capped PJM price, the backstop suspension, "meaningfully lower" Texas forward curves, NRG tracking below its guidance midpoint on softer Texas prices and a $70 million Virginia carbon-market charge, and a rotation out of 2025's winners [U1-43] [U1-48] [U1-40]. Per-share sensitivity to $1 per MWh of open power price is about $0.90 for Talen, $0.35 for Vistra and $0.40–0.60 for Constellation (U1 estimates). Talen is the most levered, Constellation the most contracted; 2028 is the open year for all of them.

4.3 Nuclear contracts are a pricing story, not a supply story, and the islanded co-location model is dead

About 9.2 GW of US nuclear output is under hyperscaler or large-corporate contract (the G2 report's estimate), at premiums that Constellation itself frames as "$20 to $50 a megawatt-hour" over market [G2-7] [G2-11], but almost all of it is existing output re-labelled; the net-new supply before 2030 is the three restarts and about 2,400 MW of uprate applications expected through 2032 [U1-76] [U1-39]. The regulator settled the structure: it rejected the behind-the-meter arrangement at Susquehanna and refused to reconsider [G2-95], Talen moved its Amazon contract to a grid-connected supply through PPL's wires, and PJM's proposed co-location services, filed to take effect in July 2026, cap behind-the-meter generation at 50 MW [U1-33] [U1-31] [U1-32]. Texas went further and ruled that a co-located load can be curtailed without regard to its paired generator [U1-11]. The winners of that transition are the owners of already-interconnected plants and the wires utilities that carry the power; the losers are the "island a data center on a reactor" plans.

4.4 Regulated utilities: load lowers bills in Texas and the Midwest, and raises them in Virginia and Georgia

Affordability politics is the real 2026–27 risk for regulated names, and it is already differentiating them. Virginia's regulator cut Dominion's requested return from 10.4% to 9.8% and removed about $350 million of speculative data-center costs; Exelon withdrew a Pennsylvania rate request; Georgia bills rose $43 a month in two years and the Public Service Commission faces elections [U1-16] [U1-35] [U1-58]. In the other direction, CenterPoint projects at least $5 billion of delivery-charge reductions for other customers from new load in Houston, Alliant says growth keeps Iowa rates flat through at least 2029, and Entergy cites $7 billion of cumulative customer benefits from its industrial load growth [U1-67] [U1-69] [U1-60]. The sector trades at 14–20 times 2027 earnings near 52-week lows; the discount is for politics and equity issuance, not for a shortage of load. Within it, the wires-heavy names with tariff-backed load (PPL, Sempra's Oncor, FirstEnergy) carry the least political risk, because federal transmission rates are formulaic and Virginia-style direct assignment protects the rate base.

4.5 Gas is the marginal fuel, and the constraint is pipe, not gas

Estimates of incremental gas demand from data centers have roughly tripled in eighteen months, from 4–6 billion cubic feet a day (Bcf/d) by 2030 to BloombergNEF's 15 Bcf/d by 2035 and Wood Mackenzie's 17 Bcf/d of power-sector growth, against about 107 Bcf/d of current US production; the Energy Information Administration still forecasts gas at $3.28 per million British thermal units in 2027, so the molecule is not yet priced [U2-3] [U2-12] [U2-7]. The pipe is. Every data-center-linked interstate project lands in 2027–29: Transco's 1.6 Bcf/d Southeast expansion in the fourth quarter of 2027, Kinder Morgan's Mississippi Crossing in the second quarter of 2028 and South System Expansion 4 in 2028–29, Boardwalk's Kosci Junction in the first half of 2029 [U2-19] [U2-1] [U2-50]. EQT lists nearly 20 Bcf/d of potential Appalachian projects but warns that a third of the basin's supply "will be challenged to hold flat" without new takeaway, and October forward prices opened a $2 gap between Appalachia and the Southeast [U2-18] [U2-59]. The producers therefore get the demand but not the price; the pipeline owners get both.

4.6 Pipeline backlogs are the cleanest evidence that the demand is real

Kinder Morgan's backlog is $9.6 billion, 92% natural gas and "more than 60%" tied to power generation and utility demand [U2-1]. Williams raised its 2026 EBITDA guidance to $8.3–8.5 billion and its long-term growth target to 11% or more a year through 2030, has a 2.6 GW behind-the-meter power portfolio funded by a $5.34 billion Blackstone joint venture, and reports a "6 GW+" power backlog [U2-5] [U2-48]. DT Midstream has a $3.4 billion backlog and counts 7.5 Bcf/d of potential gas demand from 50 GW of large loads announced by Midwest utilities; TC Energy has two Columbia expansions explicitly for data-center generation; Enbridge's C$41 billion secured backlog includes 1.4 GW of renewables and 1.6 GWh of batteries contracted to Meta [U2-10] [U2-8] [U2-38]. These backlogs rose in every second-quarter report the research read, while the shares of most of the group fell.

4.7 Behind-the-meter power moved from pilot to portfolio, and the compression companies are the quiet beneficiaries

Williams delivered first power at the 200 MW Socrates plant for Meta in Ohio in under 18 months and has 2.6 GW of similar plants in hand [U2-5]. Kodiak Gas Services, a compression lessor, bought a 405 MW power fleet, ordered 1 GW of gas turbines for delivery by 2030, targets 2 GW, and on 21 September 2026 signed a 76 MW, six-year primary-power contract for a West Texas data center [U2-9] [U2-44]. The engineering point is that compression companies already own the gas-engine service network and the turbine allocations that an onsite plant needs; the "quiet" beneficiaries are the ones with Caterpillar and turbine slots, not the ones with the best slides. For utilities, behind-the-meter power is a threat to load forecasts, not to 2026–29 earnings: about 90 GW is announced and about 2 GW operates [U1-20].

4.8 Flexible data centers are the unpriced engineering transition

Duke University's Nicholas Institute estimated that 76 GW of new load could be added to the existing US grid if data centers curtailed about 0.5% of their hours [U2-42]. Google has embedded 1 GW of demand response into agreements with five utilities; Georgia Power's 3.2 GW, 25-year contract with OpenAI includes 1 GW of flexible demand, the first such clause Southern has codified; PJM's connect-and-manage and curtail-first rules take effect in June 2027; Texas mandates curtailment [U1-77] [U1-56] [U1-28] [U1-11]. Flexible, partly curtailable load is worth more to a capacity-short grid than firm load: it lowers the generation a utility must build while preserving the wires rate base, and it reduces the scarcity rent of peaking plants. That favors transmission-and-distribution utilities and slightly hurts peaker owners, and it is in nobody's capital plan yet.

4.9 Outside the United States, the grid connection is the product, and regulators are now pricing it

Ireland requires any new data center above 10 megavolt-amperes to bring 100% matching generation; TenneT says large parts of Noord-Holland, including Amsterdam's business district, have no new grid capacity for at least ten years; Britain's demand connection queue tripled to 125 GW and is being re-ordered with deposits; Hydro-Québec proposed a 13-cent-per-kilowatt-hour data-center rate, about double its large-industrial rate; Korea is creating eleven regional price zones while its utility carries ₩202 trillion of debt [U2-32] [U2-46] [U2-33] [U2-49] [U2-34]. Only a handful of non-US providers have contracted, dated data-center load: Capital Power's 250 MW for Meta in Alberta from the second half of 2028, TAQA's 1 GW plant, financed explicitly to serve Abu Dhabi's AI strategy under a state offtake contract, YTL Power's planned 1.2 GW campus in Johor, and RWE's two European deals "nearing agreement" [U2-17] [U2-53] [U2-37] [U2-30]. Everyone else has generic demand growth that is real but neither contracted nor dated. Europe's AI capacity will be smaller, later and more expensive than America's, which is good for the few owners of connected sites and bad for almost everyone else.

Prices are the closes of Friday 2 October 2026 for every listing, with two exceptions where they occur: Saudi listings use 1 October 2026 (the Tadawul is shut on Fridays), and Kazatomprom's London line uses the 2 October close reported by stockanalysis.com because Yahoo's chart feed had not posted that day's bar. Market values are converted to US dollars at the 2 October 2026 closing exchange rates.

Independent ratings and targets come from the Yahoo Finance consensus feed, which carries data from LSEG (the London Stock Exchange Group) and S&P Global, retrieved on 2 October 2026. The rating mean runs from 1 (strong buy) to 5 (sell); "No label" means the feed showed no rating although targets exist. Consensus earnings for Enbridge and TC Energy are in Canadian dollars and were converted to US dollars for their New York lines; Capital Power is quoted and estimated in Canadian dollars. For regulated utilities the consensus target reflects a yield-plus-growth model, so a 15–25% implied upside is normal and should not be read as a strong signal. "From high" is the distance below the highest daily close of the past 52 weeks.

Claude's 12-month target is a stated multiple multiplied by next fiscal year's consensus earnings per share, rounded. The fair-value range is ±10% around it. Choosing the multiple is the judgment call, explained in each card: I use lower multiples where earnings look like a cyclical peak, higher ones for durable franchises and for cyclical companies whose earnings are still near the bottom of their cycle, and I took into account a 10-year US Treasury yield of 5.28% (2 October 2026 close). "Would buy below" applies the same method with a stricter multiple, rounded down to a round number (the nearest dollar below $100, the nearest $5 below $1,000, and so on). Ratings: Buy = high conviction that the shares are worth more than the price; Accumulate = positive, but with more risk or less upside than a Buy; Speculative buy = attractive only if a risky part of the thesis works, with a real chance of large losses; Hold = fairly priced; Avoid = the risks or the price outweigh the thesis. A rating combines the upside with my conviction and the risks, so a low-risk Buy can show less upside than an Accumulate.

Glossary

Short explanations of the technical and financial terms used on this page.

Hyperscaler
One of the largest cloud and internet companies (Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta, Oracle and their peers), which build data centers measured in gigawatts and sign the largest power contracts.
Combined-cycle plant, peaker
A combined-cycle plant burns gas in a turbine and uses the exhaust heat to drive a steam turbine, reaching about 60% efficiency; it runs most hours. A peaker (peaking plant) is a simpler, less efficient plant that runs only in the hours of highest demand and earns most of its money from capacity payments and price spikes.
RTO
A regional transmission organization, the independent operator that runs the grid and the wholesale markets for a region. PJM is an RTO; "RTO-wide" means the price that applies across its whole footprint.
Forward curve
The set of prices at which electricity or gas can be bought today for delivery in future months and years. A "lower forward curve" means the market expects lower prices ahead.
Merger arbitrage, spread
Buying the shares of a company that has agreed to be acquired at a fixed price. The spread is the gap between the market price and the deal price; a spread under 1% means the market treats the deal as nearly certain and leaves little return.
Bcfe
Billion cubic feet equivalent: a producer's gas and liquids output expressed as if it were all gas.
EPC
Engineering, procurement and construction: the contractor that designs and builds a power plant. NRG's "turbine and EPC capacity" means it has reserved both the machines and the builders.
Junior subordinated notes
Long-dated debt that ranks behind a company's other debt and is partly treated as equity by rating agencies, which lets a company raise money without issuing shares.
Nuclear production tax credit
A federal credit (from the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act) paid to existing reactors when power prices fall below a threshold, which acts as a floor under their revenue.
GS-5
Dominion Energy Virginia's new rate class for the largest customers (data centers), effective 1 January 2027, with 14-year contracts and collateral requirements.
Capacity interconnection rights
The right, attached to a plant's grid connection, to sell a given amount of capacity into PJM's market. Crane inherited rights from retired coal units, which is why its restart needed federal approval.
MVA
Megavolt-ampere, the unit of a grid connection's rated size; for most data-center purposes it is close to a megawatt.
Regulated utility, rate base, allowed return
A regulated utility earns an allowed return (about 9.6–9.8% in recent US decisions) on its rate base, the capital it has invested in wires and plants that regulators let it recover from customers. Growth comes from investing more, not from selling at higher prices.
Merchant generator, independent power producer
A company that sells electricity into wholesale markets at market prices, unlike a regulated utility. Constellation, Vistra, Talen and NRG are the large US examples.
Transmission and distribution ('wires') utility
A utility that owns the lines and substations but little or no generation. Transmission rates are set by the federal regulator using formulas; distribution rates by state commissions.
PJM, ERCOT, MISO, ISO-NE
Regional grid operators. PJM runs the grid for 13 eastern states and Washington, DC; ERCOT runs most of Texas; MISO runs the Midwest and parts of the South; ISO-NE runs New England.
FERC, PUC, NRC
The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission regulates interstate transmission, wholesale markets and pipelines. Public utility commissions are the state regulators. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission licenses reactors.
Capacity market, Base Residual Auction, $/MW-day
PJM pays generators to be available in a future delivery year through an annual auction. The price is quoted per megawatt per day: $325/MW-day is about $119,000 per MW per year.
UCAP
Unforced capacity: a plant's capacity after a haircut for expected outages, which is what clears in the auction.
Price cap and collar
Limits placed on PJM's auction price in 2025 under a settlement with Pennsylvania; the cap of about $325/MW-day runs through the 2029/30 auction.
Reliability backstop procurement
A proposed one-time PJM auction for 15-year contracts to build new generation for data-center load, capped at $555/MW-day. The federal regulator suspended it on 29 September 2026.
Delivery year
PJM's planning year, running from 1 June to 31 May; the 2028/29 delivery year begins on 1 June 2028.
Interconnection queue, large-load request
The list of generators or large customers asking to connect to the grid. Requests far exceed what gets built; collateral requirements now separate the real ones.
Take-or-pay, minimum bill, large-load tariff
Contract terms that make a data center pay for a large share of its contracted capacity whether or not it uses it, usually for 10–15 years, often with collateral. Twenty-five states have approved such tariffs.
Co-location, behind the meter, front of the meter
Co-location places a data center next to a power plant. Behind-the-meter supply bypasses the grid; front-of-the-meter supply flows through the grid under a contract. Regulators have pushed nuclear deals to the front-of-the-meter model.
Power purchase agreement (PPA)
A long-term contract to buy electricity at an agreed price, such as Microsoft's 20-year contract for the Crane reactor.
Hedge ratio
The share of expected generation already sold forward at fixed prices. Vistra is about 94% hedged for 2027; Talen about 70%.
Uprate, restart
An uprate raises an existing reactor's licensed output by tens of megawatts; a restart brings a closed reactor back into service.
Demand response, flexible load, curtailment
Arrangements under which a customer reduces its consumption when the grid is tight, in exchange for faster connection or payment. Google has embedded 1 GW of it in its utility agreements.
Bcf/d, MMcf/d
Billion and million cubic feet of natural gas per day. One Bcf/d burned in modern combined-cycle plants produces roughly 6–7 GW of continuous power (my arithmetic).
Henry Hub, basis
Henry Hub is the US benchmark gas price. Basis is the difference between a regional price and Henry Hub; Appalachian gas trades at a discount because the basin lacks pipeline capacity.
Midstream, takeaway, interstate and intrastate pipelines
Midstream companies gather, process and transport gas. Takeaway is pipeline capacity out of a producing basin. Interstate pipelines need a federal certificate (20–36 months); intrastate pipelines within Texas do not.
Compression
Gas must be compressed to move through pipelines and into plants; compression companies lease the engine-driven compressors and own the field-service workforce.
Final investment decision (FID)
The point at which a company commits capital to build a project.
LNG
Liquefied natural gas, exported by ship. US export terminals compete with power plants for the same Gulf Coast gas.
Levelized cost of energy
The average cost per MWh over a plant's life. Lazard's 2026 study puts combined-cycle gas at $51–129, utility solar at $40–98 and new nuclear at $175–255 per MWh (U2 report, source 39).
Clean Transition Tariff
A Nevada utility tariff under which a large customer pays for new clean firm generation (such as Ormat's geothermal for Google) built for it.
Capacity factor
The share of maximum output a plant actually produces over a year. US nuclear plants typically run above 90%.
EBITDA, EV/EBITDA, free-cash-flow yield
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a proxy for operating cash flow. Enterprise value (market value plus net debt) divided by EBITDA is the usual multiple for generators and pipelines. Free-cash-flow yield is free cash flow divided by market value.
EPS, forward P/E, dividend yield
Earnings per share; the forward price-to-earnings ratio divides the share price by next fiscal year's consensus earnings per share; the dividend yield is the annual dividend divided by the price.
2027E
The estimate for calendar 2027.
Consensus, mean score, LSEG
The average of the analysts who cover a stock: their ratings scored from 1 (strong buy) to 5 (sell), and their 12-month price targets. LSEG (the London Stock Exchange Group) and S&P Global supply the underlying estimates.
GDR, ADR, OTC line
Depositary receipts are certificates traded in London (GDR) or New York (ADR) that represent shares of a foreign company. Over-the-counter (OTC) lines trade outside the main exchanges and can be thinly traded.
K-1
The US tax form issued by partnerships such as Energy Transfer instead of the usual dividend statement; it complicates tax filing for US holders.
n/m
Not meaningful, shown where earnings are negative.
