What this is

This section was written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, at the request of the site's owner. It is an independent second opinion that sits alongside the pages built by the Grok section bots. The work had three parts. First, three parallel audits read every page, diagram, figure and data file on the site, checking which relevant companies are missing, which tickers or facts are wrong, and which statements are out of date. They found 587 items, 110 of them high priority. Second, nine research agents each wrote a dated, sourced report on one part of the value chain: power inside the data hall, power semiconductors, grid and facility equipment, thermal generation (turbines, engines and fuel cells), nuclear power and its fuel chain, United States electricity providers and power markets, the gas chain and international providers, lasers and photonic materials, and copper versus co-packaged optics. Third, I checked those reports against each other, pulled consistent snapshots of prices and analyst consensus for about 300 listings (including names considered and rejected; about 130 companies appear on the pages), and made the final calls on the four pages below.

The pages are grouped in two parts. Power has three subsections that follow the electricity from where it is sold to where it is used: Electrification covers the equipment between the grid connection and the chip (transformers, switchgear, direct-current distribution, power semiconductors); Generation covers the machines that make the electricity (gas turbines and engines, fuel cells, nuclear restarts and small modular reactors, and the uranium, enrichment and forging chain behind them); and Energy providers covers the companies that sell the electricity and the fuel (regulated utilities, merchant generators and nuclear plant owners, gas pipelines and compression, and the non-US providers). Interconnects is one page: copper versus photonics in AI clusters, from the raw materials to co-packaged optics.

All four pages follow the same structure: a short analysis of the field, the engineering and market realities that I think the market has not priced, a bottleneck scorecard, a list of what is already priced or over-hyped, and then the picks. Each pick shows what the company makes, why that product matters, why this company, dated evidence, risks, the analyst consensus (rating with mean, high and low targets) and my own rating and 12-month target with its basis. Every page ends with a glossary of the technical and financial terms it uses.

Seven conclusions that cut across the four pages

1. The market marked down the bottleneck owners while their order books grew

Many owners of the scarcest steps trade 25–55% below their 2026 highs. In electrification and interconnects: HD Hyundai Electric (transformers, −52%), Mitsui Kinzoku (copper foil for circuit boards, −53%), Nittobo (glass cloth for circuit boards, −47%), Powell (medium-voltage switchgear, −41%), onsemi (power chips, −40%), BE Semiconductor Industries, or BESI (chip-bonding tools, −40%), Aixtron (tools that grow lasers, −39%) and Infineon (power chips, −32%). In generation: INNIO (gas engines, −51%), BWX Technologies (reactor components, −43%), Cameco (uranium and a 49% stake in Westinghouse, −36%), Japan Steel Works (reactor forgings, −31%) and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (turbines, −27%). Among the electricity sellers: Constellation (−36%), Vistra (−34%), Talen (−28%) and NRG (−48%), all of which raised their guidance in 2026, and Kodiak Gas Services (compression and onsite power, −29%). Over the same period their backlogs and the third-party shortage estimates rose. Several things drove the shares down. Interest rates rose: the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate by a quarter point in September (CNBC), and the US 10-year Treasury yield closed at 5.28% on 2 October 2026. A report on 18 August of about $3 trillion of off-balance-sheet AI commitments hit power-equipment shares (Yahoo Finance). On 14 September AI-lab leaders called for a development slowdown, and the PHLX Semiconductor Index, the main US index of chip stocks, fell 5.9% that day (Reuters via Investing.com). For the electricity sellers, rule changes did the rest: a capped PJM capacity price, a suspended backstop auction and mandatory curtailment in Texas. Yet the semiconductor index is still up about 85% this year and only about 10% below its closing high (Yahoo Finance data), so the markdowns are concentrated in industrial, materials and energy suppliers rather than in the chip leaders. That gap is where most of my picks come from.

2. Scarcity has moved upstream into materials, certification, castings and connections

The binding constraints of 2026–28 are less often the famous systems and more often their inputs. In electrification they are extra-high-voltage transformers and their bushings (the insulated feed-throughs that carry conductors into the transformer), certified protection equipment that can safely interrupt direct current, and electrical-code approval. In generation they are the single-crystal castings for turbine blades, the engine blocks and service networks for onsite power, the heavy forgings for reactor vessels (three companies in the world), and high-assay low-enriched uranium, the fuel that most small modular reactors need and that no Western plant yet produces at scale. For the electricity sellers the scarce good is the grid connection itself, not the energy: Texas has 438 gigawatts of connection requests against falling forward power prices, and the interstate gas pipe into the Southeast is fully booked until 2028. In interconnects they are ultra-smooth copper foil, low-expansion glass cloth, the indium-phosphide (InP) wafers that lasers for fiber links are built on, and the epitaxy tools that grow the lasers' crystal layers. Many of these owners are Japanese, Korean or Canadian industrials with thin US analyst coverage and no liquid US share line. That is part of why they are mispriced, and also why they are less convenient to buy.

3. The transitions are real, but slower than the narrative, and the slowness helps the suppliers

Direct-current power in the data hall arrives in three steps: "sidecar" racks in 2026, which sit beside the servers and convert alternating current to direct current for a whole row; native 800-volt racks in 2027–28; and facility-wide DC after about 2029. Generation follows the same pattern. Onsite gas engines and small turbines carry the 2026–28 build because heavy turbines are sold out to 2030–31 and the grid arrives in 2029–31; "bridge" power is becoming permanent. Nuclear adds about 3 gigawatts of net-new US supply before 2030, almost all from three restarts, and the first small modular reactor delivers power in 2030–32 at the earliest, so the nuclear contracts signed in 2025–26 mostly re-label existing output. Optics in AI clusters also arrives in steps: co-packaged optics already ships in scale-out switches, optical scale-up links come between racks in 2027–28 and inside the rack only in 2029–30. A slower transition extends the life of copper boards, gas engines, existing reactors and existing pipelines, which is where the earnings are.

4. Rules, not demand, set the 2026–27 returns for the companies that sell electricity

The grid operator for 13 eastern states, PJM, now sells capacity at an administered price: three auctions in a row cleared at the cap ($325–333 per megawatt-day), the cap runs through the 2029/30 auction, and the federal regulator suspended PJM's one-time auction for 15-year contracts on 29 September 2026. Texas approved a fast connection process but ruled that a data center next to a power plant can still be fully curtailed. Twenty-five states have approved tariffs that make data centers pay for most of their contracted capacity whether they use it or not. Each of these rules moved more share prices in 2026 than any change in contracted demand did. The merchant generators are hedged through 2027, so the open questions are 2028 and the first uncapped PJM auction in May 2027. For regulated utilities the rule that matters is who pays: in Texas and the Midwest new load lowers other customers' bills and regulators welcome it; in Virginia, Georgia and Pennsylvania it raises them and the returns are being cut.

5. Only demand with collateral behind it is worth counting

Connection queues are roughly six times any plausible build: 438 gigawatts in Texas, 60 gigawatts of utility forecasts in PJM that the operator cut to 34 before publishing, and a 40% fall in Exelon's "high-probability" load once customers had to post security. Behind-the-meter power plants are announced at about 90 gigawatts and operating at about 2. About 9 gigawatts of nuclear output is under hyperscaler contract, but almost all of it was already generating. The numbers that survive are the contracts with collateral: roughly 100–120 gigawatts of US utility load under signed agreements with collateral or minimum bills (the U1 report's estimate, summing the utilities' contracted figures and removing double counting), 75 gigawatts of firm orders for onsite generation, and order books at the turbine, transformer, pipeline and compression companies that rose in every second-quarter report. My picks are built on those numbers, not on the queues.

6. The popular expressions are priced

GE Vernova (about 40× 2027 earnings), Bloom Energy (about 58×), Williams (about 27×, the highest multiple in US midstream), Lumentum (at its high, about 30× next fiscal year's earnings and 48× this year's), Astera Labs (about 56×), Soitec (up about 580% this year) and the capacitor makers (Samsung Electro-Mechanics up about 520%) already price the story. The small-modular-reactor developers (Oklo, NuScale, X-energy, NANO Nuclear) have no earnings and fell 60–86% from their highs while the first US construction permit was issued; the fuel and component suppliers are the better expression. Several popular narratives also run ahead of the engineering. Solid-state transformers will not earn meaningful revenue before about 2029. Optical versions of NVLink, NVIDIA's GPU-to-GPU link, will not reach inside the rack in 2027. Glass-core package substrates have slipped beyond 2026–27. Hybrid bonding will not arrive with the HBM4 generation of stacked memory. Co-locating a data center on a reactor's own switchyard is finished as a model; the regulators have pushed every such deal onto the grid.

7. The main risks are shared

Six risks hit all four fields at once. The first is a pause in AI capital spending: the hyperscalers (the largest cloud companies) still guide to record 2026 spending, but September brought public calls for a slowdown, and the electricity sellers would feel it in 2028, the first year that is not hedged. The second is higher interest rates, which hit the utilities and pipelines directly. The third is a slip in NVIDIA's roadmap, such as its next rack design, Kyber, moving to 2028. The fourth is China and trade policy: export licensing of indium, which laser makers need; a suspension of China's gallium export ban that expires around late November 2026; a trade truce extended only to 10 January 2027; the Pentagon's Section 1260H list, which now includes the largest optical-transceiver maker, InnoLight; and the end of waivers for Russian enriched uranium on 1 January 2028. The fifth is regulation and politics in electricity: a PJM cap extended again, a weak May 2027 auction, or affordability rulings that cut allowed returns. The sixth is an overbuild in 2028–31: turbine capacity rising from about 54 to 80–85 gigawatts a year, contracted gas plants arriving as flexible data centers reduce the need for them, and in interconnects transformers, indium-phosphide wafers, low-power lasers and silicon-photonics foundries.

What the site audit found

The audits read every page, every clickable diagram stage and every data file, and checked time-sensitive facts against primary sources where they could. They produced 587 items (110 high, 253 medium and 224 low priority), each with the file, the exact location, the action (add, fix, move, remove or link), the reason and a source. The most important are below. The full list is in coverage-gaps.json, and the notes for the Grok team explain how to use it. The audit describes the site as it was on 2 October 2026 (commit 9d8ec4e); between 2 and 3 October the section bots pushed about twenty commits that close many of these items, so this list is the record of what was found rather than a current to-do list.

  • Wrong tickers or links.
    • ABB is shown as NYSE-listed and linked to Fidelity as "ABB". It trades on the SIX Swiss Exchange, and its US over-the-counter line is ABBNY.
    • Schneider Electric's "SNEXF" does not exist; its US over-the-counter lines are SBGSY and SBGSF.
    • SCREEN Holdings is shown as 6305.T, which is Hitachi Construction Machinery; SCREEN is 7735.T.
    • SK Siltron is shown as 008500.KS, a car-seat-fabric maker; SK Siltron is unlisted.
    • The Fidelity link for ASM International opens a silver miner; it should use ASMIY.
    • The Power Map's link builder turns exchange-prefixed tickers such as "LSE: CWR" (London) and "KRX: 336260" (Korea) into links for the exchange code instead of the company.
  • Corporate events not reflected.
    • New listings: Cerebras (CBRS), SK hynix's US share line (SKHY), SpaceX/xAI (SPCX) and Zhipu (2513.HK).
    • Changes of ownership: Altera is now standalone under Silver Lake, CoolIT belongs to Ecolab, DuPont's electronics business became Qnity (Q), Synopsys owns Ansys, and NVIDIA's purchase of Hugging Face is pending.
    • Wrong attributions: the Reinhausen solid-state-transformer partnership is Siemens AG's, not Siemens Energy's. ABB no longer supplies high-voltage direct-current (HVDC) links, since that business is now Hitachi Energy. The "NextEra–Dominion package" is an acquisition, not a partnership.
  • Stale or inconsistent data.
    • Every 2026 capital-spending figure on the Hyperscalers page is out of date. Current guidance is Amazon about $220 billion, Alphabet $195–205 billion, Meta $130–145 billion and Oracle's fiscal 2027 $90–95 billion.
    • Dollar amounts were stripped from text in the public data files ("Cloud revenue 0B").
    • The Oklo tracker shows stale gauges.
    • The master company file holds 87 records, while the pages show about 174 tickers.
  • Coverage gaps in core sections.
    • Power: no battery-storage stage at all, and missing generator-set makers (Cummins, Generac, Rolls-Royce mtu), Korean transformer makers, high-voltage cable makers and Lite-On.
    • Hardware: no circuit-board, laminate or copper-foil layer, even though it is the copper bottleneck.
    • Photonics: no Asian transceiver or laser makers (InnoLight, Eoptolink, Sumitomo Electric, Yuanjie) and no makers of the indium-phosphide wafers that lasers are built on. The co-packaged-optics pages show Broadcom's older Bailly switch instead of its current Tomahawk 6-Davisson.
    • Fabrication: missing packaging and test tools (DISCO, BESI, Hanmi, ASMPT), substrates (Ibiden, Unimicron, Ajinomoto) and Aixtron.
    • Software: missing major security and data-infrastructure names.
    • Hyperscalers: missing the Chinese clouds, most "neoclouds" (specialist GPU-cloud companies), the bitcoin miners that now lease power and space to AI customers, and the data-center real-estate investment trusts (REITs).

Method, data and caveats

Prices. Two price snapshots are in use, and each page states which. The electrification and interconnect pages use the latest full-session closes available on the morning of 2 October 2026, New York time: 1 October 2026 for US and European listings, 2 October 2026 for Tokyo, Seoul, Taipei and Hong Kong, and 30 September 2026 for mainland China; their market values are converted to US dollars at mid-morning New York exchange rates on 2 October. The generation and energy-provider pages use the closes of Friday 2 October 2026 for every listing (Saudi listings 1 October; Kazatomprom's London line from stockanalysis.com), with market values at the 2 October closing exchange rates. The close date is shown under each price in the table above and in the data file.

Independent ratings and targets come from the Yahoo Finance consensus feed, which carries data from LSEG (the London Stock Exchange Group) and S&P Global, retrieved on 2 October 2026. The rating mean runs from 1 (strong buy) to 5 (sell); "No label" means the feed showed no rating although targets exist. Consensus targets for Asian companies can lag recent broker changes. "From high" is the distance below the highest daily close of the past 52 weeks.

Claude's 12-month target is a stated multiple multiplied by next fiscal year's consensus earnings per share, rounded. The fair-value range is ±10% around it. Choosing the multiple is the judgment call, explained in each card: I use lower multiples where earnings look like a cyclical peak, higher ones for durable franchises and for cyclical companies whose earnings are still near the bottom of their cycle, and I took into account a 10-year US Treasury yield of 5.24–5.28% (the 1 and 2 October 2026 closes). "Would buy below" applies the same method with a stricter multiple, rounded down to a round number (the nearest dollar below $100, the nearest $5 below $1,000, and so on). Ratings: Buy = high conviction that the shares are worth more than the price; Accumulate = positive, but with more risk or less upside than a Buy; Speculative buy = attractive only if a risky part of the thesis works, with a real chance of large losses; Hold = fairly priced; Avoid = the risks or the price outweigh the thesis. A rating combines the upside with my conviction and the risks, so a low-risk Buy can show less upside than an Accumulate.

Research process. Nine research agents wrote value-chain reports: in-hall DC power; power semiconductors; grid and facility equipment; thermal and onsite generation; nuclear, small modular reactors and the fuel cycle; US utilities and merchant generators; gas supply, midstream and non-US providers; lasers and photonic materials; and copper, co-packaged optics and optical I/O. Each report gives every figure with its date and source. I then checked the reports against each other, re-pulled all prices and consensus data in one consistent snapshot, and made the final calls myself. Figures marked "my estimate" are my arithmetic on stated assumptions. Sources marked "secondary" are press relays or aggregators.
