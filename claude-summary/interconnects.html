1. The short version

Data moves through an AI cluster over three tiers. Scale-up links join accelerators into one large virtual GPU, as NVIDIA's NVLink interconnect does inside a rack, and today they are copper. Scale-out links connect racks into a cluster over Ethernet or InfiniBand. They use pluggable optical transceivers, and increasingly co-packaged optics on the switch. Scale-across links connect data-center buildings and campuses with coherent optics, a long-distance transmission technique that encodes data in the phase as well as the brightness of light (it is unrelated to the company Coherent). The investment debate is about when optics replaces copper and how deep into the chip package the optics goes. NVIDIA's own roadmap keeps copper inside the rack through its 2027–28 generation (the Rubin Ultra GPU and the Kyber rack). Optics enters scale-up first between racks: the NVL576 system, which joins 576 GPUs across eight racks, in test volumes in 2027, then the Feynman generation's NVL1152 with co-packaged optics on NVLink in 2028. SemiAnalysis, an independent semiconductor research firm, warned in July that NVL576 is likely to be delayed or limited to small volumes [I3-14]. Optical links inside the rack are a 2029–30 event at the earliest [I3-4] [I3-8] [I3-9].

That timeline has two consequences that the market under-weights. First, copper's limit is showing up as a circuit-board materials wall. The pinch points are ultra-smooth copper foil, low-expansion glass cloth (the woven glass fiber inside circuit boards) and the M9-class laminates, the lowest-loss grade of board material, needed for 78-layer midplanes (the large boards that connect compute and switch trays inside a rack). The owners of those materials were marked down by about half while the shortage estimates got worse. Second, the binding constraint for optics in 2026–27 is indium-phosphide (InP) laser capacity, which is gated by substrates and by epitaxy, the step that grows the laser's crystal layers. The laser leader, Lumentum, trades at its high. The upstream owners (Sumitomo Electric, JX Advanced Metals and the tool maker Aixtron) trade 30–40% below their highs at about 18–27 times earnings. Lasers for fiber links are made from InP, a compound of indium and phosphorus, because silicon cannot emit light efficiently.

Co-packaged optics (CPO) places the optical engine on the switch package instead of in a pluggable module at the front panel. It is shipping in scale-out switches (NVIDIA Quantum-X and Spectrum-X Photonics, Broadcom Bailly and Tomahawk 6-Davisson), but volumes are still small. It cuts the number of lasers, but probably not the InP area or the dollars per terabit (my estimate), because its external light sources, laser modules that sit outside the package and feed light in through fiber, need roughly ten times the optical power per laser. Each step deeper into the package cuts the energy per bit. A pluggable transceiver with a signal processor uses about 20 picojoules per bit (pJ/bit; 1 pJ/bit at 1 terabit per second is 1 watt), linear pluggables (modules without a signal processor) or copper about 10, co-packaged optics about 5–7, and optical I/O chiplets (small optical chips inside the processor package) about 3–5. That gradient is the long-run engineering case for optics.

Why copper runs out. At 224 Gb/s per lane, signals run at about 112 billion symbols per second using four-level amplitude modulation. The highest frequency the link must carry (the Nyquist frequency) is about 56 GHz, and at that frequency the current flows only in a skin about 0.3 µm deep at the copper's surface (my calculation). Surface roughness of the copper foil, and the energy absorbed by the glass and resin around it (dielectric loss), therefore dominate the loss, and passive copper reaches only about 1–2 m. Going to 448 Gb/s per lane shortens the reach further; an optics-led industry white paper says "to tens of centimeters" [I3-71]. NVIDIA's Rubin avoids that for now by sending 224 Gb/s in each direction at once over the same copper pair [I3-10]. Copper in the rack survives by using much better materials: hyper-very-low-profile (HVLP) copper foil, whose grades 3, 4 and 5 are progressively smoother; glass cloth with a low dielectric constant, which absorbs less signal energy; M8 and M9 laminates, the two lowest-loss board-material grades; and boards of 70 layers or more. The fact that copper lasts longer therefore increases demand for those materials. The co-packaged-optics substrates, which are large packages that must not warp, need them too.

Why optics is not yet everywhere. Reach is not the problem for optics; energy per bit, cost per bit and reliability are. A pluggable transceiver needs a digital signal processor at each end to clean up and re-time the electrical signal after it crosses the board. Every step that moves the optics closer to the chip removes a re-timing or re-driving stage. Co-packaged optics then has to solve laser reliability (the lasers move to replaceable external light sources), fiber attachment at sub-micron precision, and testing optical engines (the photonic and electronic chips that convert between electrical and optical signals) before they are committed to an expensive package.

Each step deeper into the package removes a conversion stage. Moving from a pluggable with a signal processor to co-packaged optics saves about 3×, and moving from co-packaged optics to on-package optical I/O saves roughly another 1.5–2×. Wide-and-slow links send data over many parallel channels at modest speed. Examples are micro-LED arrays (Avicena) and links between chips built from 1,060 nm VCSELs, cheap surface-emitting lasers (Lumentum, Qualcomm and Corning). They promise transmitters below one picojoule per bit, but only over about 10–30 m. Silica's chromatic dispersion at blue wavelengths limits micro-LED reach, and the silicon is early [I2-65] [I2-8]. Inside the rack, passive copper costs only the energy of the serializer-deserializer (SerDes) circuit at each end. That is why NVIDIA keeps copper there until signalling rate, reach and board manufacturability force the change.

Optics into memory. The most explicit "optics into memory" product with a public owner is Celestial AI's Photonic Fabric. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) is stacked memory placed next to the processor, and the number of stacks is limited by the length of chip edge available for connections, the so-called beachfront. Photonic Fabric replaces that electrical edge connection with an optical chiplet and pools memory inside the switch. Marvell bought Celestial AI for about $3.5 billion (closed 2 Feb 2026), and the commonly cited revenue path is only about $150 million in fiscal 2028 [I3-26] [I3-28]. Optically disaggregated memory in production is a 2028+ story, later than optical scale-up of compute.

4. Seven engineering realities the market has not fully priced

4.1 The copper wall is a circuit-board materials wall

SemiAnalysis reported that NVIDIA's Kyber NVL144 rack slipped to 2028 because its printed-circuit-board midplane is hard to build: a 78-layer stack made of three 26-layer sections, close to 1 m², with lines and spaces of 25 µm or less for 448G-class signalling. NVIDIA replied only that its "roadmap is intact" [I3-13]. Morgan Stanley's teardown puts board content at about $116.7k per Vera Rubin NVL72 rack (NVIDIA's 2026–27 rack of 72 GPUs), against $35.1k for the current GB300 rack (relayed, secondary) [I3-52]. Goldman Sachs estimates the effective supply deficit of grade-3-and-above HVLP copper foil at 28%, 39% and 38% for 2026, 2027 and 2028, and Morgan Stanley puts the high-end glass-cloth gap at 40% in 2026 [I3-50] [I3-51]. NVIDIA reportedly bypasses laminate makers to lock foil and glass-cloth capacity more than a year ahead [I3-53] [I3-54]. Even so, the foil leader (Mitsui Kinzoku) is about 53% below its May high and the glass-cloth leader (Nittobo) about 47% below.

4.2 Co-packaged optics cuts the number of lasers, not the indium phosphide

NVIDIA says its photonic switches use "4× fewer lasers" [I2-15]. The remaining lasers are different devices, however. Lumentum's external-light-source module delivers up to about 250 mW per wavelength across eight wavelengths at about 12 W of module power [I2-7]. That implies a module-level wall-plug efficiency of roughly (8 × 0.25 W) / 12 W ≈ 17%, which makes laser power a first-order item in the power budget (my estimate). High-power continuous-wave lasers, which emit steady light for a separate modulator to encode, need millimetre-long cavities, careful heat removal and often optical isolators, so InP area and value per laser rise several-fold. Lumentum expects ultra-high-power laser revenue to reach about $50 million a quarter by the end of 2026 and its first $100 million quarter in January–March 2027 [I2-1] [I2-2]. InP dollars per terabit therefore do not fall in proportion to the laser count.

4.3 The InP chain binds, and the move to 6-inch wafers is where the advantage shifts

The shortage is a chain of four InP-specific steps: substrates (the crystal wafers), epitaxial growth of the laser's crystal layers in MOCVD reactors (metal-organic chemical vapor deposition), front-end laser fabrication (including regrowth, a second epitaxy step needed for modulated lasers, which switch themselves on and off to encode data), and reliability burn-in, in which lasers run hot for days to weed out early failures. Lumentum said in July 2026 that its laser shipments were running more than 30% below demand [I2-5]. Coherent said "fiscal 27 is basically completely booked out", with orders into 2028, and called InP capacity "our primary constraint" [I2-10]. Moving from 3-inch to 6-inch wafers quadruples area. Coherent reports that its 6-inch lines already yield better than its 3-inch lines [I2-10]. Substrate makers find large-diameter InP crystals hard to grow because the material is brittle and prone to twinning, a defect in which part of the crystal grows in a mirrored orientation.

Laser makers are insensitive to substrate prices. At $5,000 per 6-inch wafer, after a reported 250% rise [I2-22], the substrate costs only about $0.09–0.39 per good laser die (my estimate), against laser gross margins near 50%. This is the high-bandwidth-memory pattern: buyers who do not care about price, multi-year agreements and deposits. That is why JX Advanced Metals' announced "price revisions" should stick through 2027 [I2-25].

4.4 Indium licensing matters more than indium tonnage

A 6-inch InP wafer contains only about 43 g of indium (my estimate). A million such wafers a year would use about 4% of world refinery output, which was 1,100 tonnes in 2025, of which China produced about 760 tonnes [I2-41]. The constraint is not the metal. It is China's export licensing of indium, InP and the indium precursor chemicals used in epitaxy, in force since 4 February 2025 and not part of the US–China trade truce [I2-45] [I2-46]. AXT, whose crystal growth is in China, was still waiting for permits to ship to US customers in August 2026 [I2-21]. Japanese substrate makers (Sumitomo Electric, JX Advanced Metals) are the beneficiaries.

4.5 Optics in scale-up adds demand before it replaces copper

Because optics enters scale-up between racks first, the 2027–28 optical scale-up market is additional demand on top of in-rack copper, not a replacement for it. Copper content per rack is still rising: NVL72 cable cartridges, the Kyber midplane, and 1.6T active electrical cables at Meta and xAI. The copper names most exposed to optics are the active cables between network cards and the top-of-rack switches that connect each rack to the cluster, from about 2028, and the in-rack NVLink backplanes from 2029–30. Circuit-board materials and the retimers for PCIe and CXL, the standard links between processors, memory and devices inside a server, are the least exposed [I3-4] [I3-13].

4.6 Two single-source choke points are already discovered

TSMC's COUPE (Compact Universal Photonic Engine), a process that stacks an electronic chip on a photonic chip, is the only volume co-packaged-optics platform for NVIDIA and Broadcom. Its photonic-chip capacity is reported to rise from about 500 wafers a month to 15,000 by the fourth quarter of 2026 and at least 25,000 by 2028 [I3-19]. Soitec reportedly makes about 95% of the 300 mm photonics silicon-on-insulator wafers (silicon wafers with a buried insulating layer that guides light) that silicon-photonics foundries need; that share is a secondary estimate that has not been verified [I2-61]. Soitec expects that revenue to grow 2.5–3× in its fiscal 2027 [I2-62]. Both are real choke points, but the market has found them: TSMC is near its high, and Soitec's shares are up about 580% this year [X-13].

4.7 Policy is reshaping the transceiver supply chain

InnoLight, the largest maker of 800G and 1.6T transceivers (one estimate puts it at 35–40% of 800G and 50–70% of 1.6T), earned 94.8% of its first-half 2026 revenue outside China [X-4] [X-12]. On 8 June 2026 the Pentagon added it to the Section 1260H list of "Chinese military companies". Pentagon contracts with listed firms are barred from 30 June 2026, and contracts for goods that contain their products from 30 June 2027 [X-5]. The listing does not bar US investors from owning the shares. A further listing by the US Treasury (its Non-SDN Chinese Military-Industrial Complex list) would. Cloud capacity that serves defense customers may therefore need non-Chinese modules from mid-2027, a modest tailwind for Coherent, Lumentum and modules assembled by Fabrinet. A September 2026 equipment rule from the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) did not name Chinese module makers [I3-86].

Method, data and caveats

Prices are the latest full-session close available on the morning of 2 October 2026, New York time: 1 October 2026 for US and European listings, 2 October 2026 for Tokyo, Seoul, Taipei and Hong Kong, and 30 September 2026 for mainland China (closed for the National Day holiday). Market values are converted to US dollars at mid-morning New York exchange rates on 2 October 2026.

Independent ratings and targets come from the Yahoo Finance consensus feed, which carries data from LSEG (the London Stock Exchange Group) and S&P Global, retrieved on 2 October 2026. The rating mean runs from 1 (strong buy) to 5 (sell); "No label" means the feed showed no rating although targets exist. Consensus targets for Asian companies can lag recent broker changes. Mitsui Kinzoku's figures are on the basis after its 10-for-1 share split of 1 October 2026 (each old share became ten new ones). InnoLight's figures use its Shenzhen listing, which has far more analyst coverage than its Hong Kong listing. "From high" is the distance below the highest daily close of the past 52 weeks.

Claude's 12-month target is a stated multiple multiplied by next fiscal year's consensus earnings per share, rounded. The fair-value range is ±10% around it. Choosing the multiple is the judgment call, explained in each card: I use lower multiples where earnings look like a cyclical peak, higher ones for durable franchises and for cyclical companies whose earnings are still near the bottom of their cycle, and I took into account a 10-year US Treasury yield of 5.24%. "Would buy below" applies the same method with a stricter multiple. Ratings: Buy = high conviction that the shares are worth more than the price; Accumulate = positive, but with more risk or less upside than a Buy; Speculative buy = attractive only if a risky part of the thesis works, with a real chance of large losses; Hold = fairly priced; Avoid = the risks or the price outweigh the thesis. A rating combines the upside with my conviction and the risks, so a low-risk Buy can show less upside than an Accumulate.

Glossary

Short explanations of the technical and financial terms used on this page.

Scale-up, scale-out, scale-across
Scale-up links join accelerators into one large virtual GPU inside a rack or a few racks. Scale-out links connect racks into a cluster through network switches. Scale-across links connect buildings and campuses.
NVLink, NVLink Fusion, UALink
NVLink is NVIDIA's scale-up interconnect. NVLink Fusion licenses it for use with other companies' chips. UALink is an open industry alternative backed by NVIDIA's competitors.
NVIDIA systems
GB300 NVL72 is the current rack of 72 GPUs. Vera Rubin NVL72 follows in 2026–27. Rubin Ultra and the Kyber rack design come in 2027–28, NVL576 joins 576 GPUs across eight racks, and the Feynman generation's NVL1152 (2028) is expected to carry co-packaged optics on NVLink.
Pluggable transceiver
A module plugged into the front of a switch or network card that converts electrical signals to light and back. 800G and 1.6T are its speeds in gigabits and terabits per second.
Digital signal processor (DSP)
The chip inside a pluggable module that cleans up and re-times the signal. It is the module's largest power consumer.
Linear pluggable optics
Pluggable modules without a digital signal processor; they rely on the host chip's own SerDes, so they use about half the power.
Co-packaged optics (CPO)
Optical engines placed on the same package as the switch or processor chip, so the electrical path is millimetres long instead of crossing the board.
Near-packaged optics (NPO)
Optical engines placed on the board right next to the chip package rather than inside it; a step between pluggables and co-packaged optics.
Optical engine
The photonic and electronic chips that together convert signals between electrical and optical form.
Optical I/O chiplet
A small optical chip inside the processor package that sends and receives data as light directly, the deepest form of integration on this page.
External light source (ELS, ELSFP)
A laser module that sits outside the package and feeds light in through fiber, so a failed laser can be replaced. ELSFP is its standard pluggable form.
SerDes
Serializer-deserializer: the circuit at each end of a link that turns parallel data into a fast serial signal and back.
Retimer, active electrical cable (AEC)
A retimer is a chip that regenerates a signal so a copper link can reach farther. An active electrical cable is a copper cable with such chips built into its ends.
Coherent optics, 800ZR
Long-distance optical transmission that encodes data in the phase as well as the brightness of light, used between buildings and cities. 800ZR is the 800 Gb/s standard for such links. The term is unrelated to the company Coherent.
pJ/bit
Picojoules per bit: the energy needed to move one bit. At 1 terabit per second, 1 pJ/bit equals 1 watt.
Nyquist frequency, skin depth, dielectric loss
The Nyquist frequency is the highest frequency a link must carry, half the symbol rate. At such frequencies current flows only in a thin skin at the copper's surface, so surface roughness matters. Dielectric loss is the energy absorbed by the glass and resin around the copper.
HVLP copper foil (grades 3, 4 and 5)
Hyper-very-low-profile foil, the ultra-smooth copper used in high-speed boards. Higher grades are smoother and lose less signal.
Laminate, CCL, M8 and M9
The board material, sold as copper-clad laminate (CCL). Grades are ranked by signal loss; M8 and M9 are the lowest-loss grades in volume use.
Glass cloth, T-glass, low-Dk
Woven glass fiber reinforces laminates and package substrates. T-glass expands very little with heat, which limits warping. Low-Dk glass has a low dielectric constant, so it absorbs less signal energy.
Midplane, backplane
Large circuit boards that connect the compute and switch trays inside a rack.
Indium phosphide (InP)
A compound semiconductor of indium and phosphorus used to make the lasers and photodiodes for fiber links, because silicon cannot emit light efficiently.
Substrate (wafer)
The crystal disc on which chips are built. 3-, 4- and 6-inch refer to the diameter; a 6-inch wafer has four times the area of a 3-inch one.
Modulated laser (EML), continuous-wave (CW) laser
An electro-absorption modulated laser switches its own light on and off to encode data. A continuous-wave laser emits steady light that a separate silicon-photonics modulator encodes; high-power CW lasers feed co-packaged optics.
VCSEL
Vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser: a cheap laser made on gallium arsenide, used for short links.
Epitaxy, MOCVD
Epitaxy grows the crystal layers of a laser on a substrate. Metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) is the reactor type used for this.
Regrowth, burn-in, twinning
Regrowth is a second epitaxy step needed for some lasers. Burn-in runs new lasers hot for days to weed out early failures. Twinning is a crystal defect in which part of the crystal grows in a mirrored orientation.
Silicon photonics, SOI
Silicon photonics builds optical circuits in silicon with chipmaking tools. Silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafers have a buried insulating layer that guides the light.
TSMC COUPE, SoIC, CoWoS
COUPE (Compact Universal Photonic Engine) stacks an electronic chip on a photonic chip. SoIC is TSMC's process for stacking chips with hybrid bonding. CoWoS (chip-on-wafer-on-substrate) places GPUs and memory stacks side by side on one large substrate.
Hybrid bonding, thermocompression bonding, micro-bumps
Micro-bumps are tiny solder joints between stacked chips. Thermocompression bonding joins them with heat and pressure. Hybrid bonding joins chips copper-to-copper without solder, allowing far denser connections.
High-bandwidth memory (HBM), JEDEC, beachfront
HBM is stacked memory placed next to the processor; HBM4, HBM4E and HBM5 are successive generations. JEDEC is the memory-standards body. The beachfront is the length of chip edge available for connections, which limits how many memory stacks fit.
PCIe, CXL
Standard links between processors, memory and devices inside a server.
Top-of-rack switch
The network switch that connects the servers in a rack to the rest of the cluster.
Optical circuit switch
A switch that redirects beams of light with tiny mirrors without converting them to electrical signals.
Ferrules (MT, MMC)
Precision parts in fiber connectors that align many fibers at once.
Sputtering target
A plate of pure metal used to deposit thin films during chipmaking.
1260H list, NS-CMIC list
The Pentagon's Section 1260H list names "Chinese military companies" and leads to procurement bans. The US Treasury's Non-SDN Chinese Military-Industrial Complex (NS-CMIC) list would bar US persons from buying a company's securities.
ECOC, OCP, Open CPX
The European Conference on Optical Communication is a major annual optics conference. The Open Compute Project publishes open data-center hardware specifications. Open CPX is an industry group defining a standard form for co-packaged optics.
DR4, 2×FR4
Common 800G module types for reaches of about 500 m (DR4) and 2 km (2×FR4).
FCC
The Federal Communications Commission, the US regulator for communications equipment.
ITC
The US International Trade Commission, which rules on patent complaints about imported products.
EPS, forward P/E
Earnings per share. The forward price-to-earnings ratio (Fwd P/E) divides the share price by next fiscal year's consensus earnings per share.
2027E, FY3/28E, FY6/28E
2027E is the estimate for calendar 2027. FY3/28E is the estimate for the fiscal year that ends in March 2028 (the usual Japanese fiscal year), and FY6/28E for the year that ends in June 2028.
Consensus, mean score, LSEG
The average of the analysts who cover a stock: their ratings scored from 1 (strong buy) to 5 (sell), and their 12-month price targets. LSEG (the London Stock Exchange Group) and S&P Global supply the underlying estimates.
ADR, OTC line
An American depositary receipt is a US-traded certificate that represents shares of a foreign company. Over-the-counter (OTC) lines trade outside the main exchanges and can be thinly traded.
n/m
Not meaningful, shown where earnings are negative.
