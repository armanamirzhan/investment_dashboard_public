AI Data-Center Electrification: Generators through GPU Voltage Regulators

A single-file map of how electricity reaches AI silicon—offsite Combined-Cycle Gas Turbine (CCGT) and grid paths, onsite fuel cells and turbines, hydrogen round-trip math, Solid-State Transformer (SST) versus legacy Alternating Current (AC) conversion, and in-hall Direct Current (DC) distribution to Voltage Regulators (VRs).

Prepared for Arman A Hub section · interactive terms Not investment advice

1. Overview: the AI data-center power problem

AI training and inference clusters draw continuous multi-megawatt loads per hall. The constraint is rarely “enough electrons somewhere on Earth”—it is time-to-power at a specific site, plus conversion losses and heat from each hop between generation and the sub-1 V rails on Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) and Central Processing Unit (CPU) packages.

Two high-level routes exist:

  • Offsite generation + grid: utility-scale plants (typically CCGT today) deliver power over High-Voltage Alternating Current (HVAC) or High-Voltage Direct Current (HVDC) transmission to the site boundary, then facility transformers and conversion stages feed the hall.
  • Onsite generation: fuel cells—especially Solid Oxide Fuel Cell (SOFC)—gas turbines, diesel or gas gensets, or hybrids sit inside or adjacent to the campus fence, trading capital and fuel logistics for faster interconnect and sometimes better control of Direct Current (DC) output.
Core trade-off

CCGT remains the cheapest long-run kilowatt-hour (kWh) when turbines and interconnects are available (~60–64% electrical Lower Heating Value / LHV). Fuel cells often win on time-to-power: a month early on a 1 GW AI campus can be worth on the order of ~$1–2 B of compute revenue—figures that dominate small efficiency gaps.

Inside the building, the industry is shifting from a many-stage AC chain (Uninterruptible Power Supply → Power Distribution Unit → Power Supply Unit → 12 V → VRs) toward medium-voltage AC rectified or SST-converted to ~800 V DC (or ±400 V bipolar), then rack/blade conversion and multiphase VRs. Collapsing stages cuts heat, parts count, and roughly ~5% conversion loss versus many AC–DC hops.

2. Offsite generation + grid path (CCGT, HVAC / HVDC)

Utility-scale Combined-Cycle Gas Turbine (CCGT) plants convert pipeline natural gas to electricity at roughly 60–64% LHV electrical efficiency—still the cheapest long-run kWh if you can secure the turbine and the interconnect. Power then travels over transmission:

  • High-Voltage Alternating Current (HVAC): roughly ~5–8% loss per 1 000 km class.
  • High-Voltage Direct Current (HVDC): roughly ~3.5% per 1 000 km plus ~1% per converter station.
  • Shorter hops: transmission often cited ~98.5% per ~100 km class; step-up and step-down transformers each ~99.5%.

HVDC converter stations are expensive because they switch hundreds of kilovolts down toward medium voltage—a different product class from a data-center SST that lands at ~800 V.

3. Onsite generation subtypes

Onsite options trade fuel logistics and stack/turbine capital for shorter interconnect queues and, for some fuel cells, native DC output that aligns with 800 V hall architectures after boost conversion.

3.1 Solid Oxide Fuel Cell (SOFC)

Solid Oxide Fuel Cell (SOFC) systems reform pipeline natural gas on-site and produce DC electricity. Operating temperature is high (~800 °C for stationary stacks such as Bloom’s class). Electrical efficiency is often cited in the ~55–65% LHV band; Combined Heat and Power (CHP) recovery can raise total useful energy further when heat sinks exist.

  • Bloom Energy (NYSE: BE): roughly ~55–65% electrical LHV; ~75–85% share of stationary SOFC; stack fade ~2.4 percentage points/year; stack swap cadence ~5.5 years. Electrolyte uses scandium-stabilized zirconia (ScSZ). Scandium oxide supply (~40–80 t/yr global) is a Bloom-specific scarce-material concern; Ceres and most others avoid ScSZ.
  • Ceres Power (LSE: CWR) SteelCell: steel substrate; gadolinium-doped ceria plus thin yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ); 450–630 °C; claims ~65%. Licenses to Doosan Fuel Cell (KRX: 336260; SOFC-NG claims ~64%, 50 MW/yr plant), Delta, Weichai.
  • Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (TSE: 7011) MEGAMIE: SOFC ~210 kW at ~53%—also a large-frame gas-turbine and genset supplier (see hybrid / turbines).
Degradation caveat

SOFC claims vary by product and duty cycle. Year-5 degraded SOFC at ~45% electrical can drop a modeled gas-to-chip path to ~37%, while a grid/CCGT path stays comparatively flat. At 57% SOFC uses roughly ~15% more gas per core kWh versus a matched higher-efficiency case.

3.2 Molten Carbonate Fuel Cell (MCFC)

Molten Carbonate Fuel Cell (MCFC) systems (notable public name: FuelCell Energy, NASDAQ: FCEL) typically deliver ~50% electrical / ~85% Combined Heat and Power (CHP). Construction leans on nickel and steel. They can consume carbon dioxide (CO2) as part of the cathode chemistry. Siemens has a data-center partnership context (details and commercial status may evolve—treat as partnership, not guaranteed fleet deployment).

3.3 Phosphoric Acid Fuel Cell (PAFC)

Phosphoric Acid Fuel Cell (PAFC): HyAxiom PureCell (private; possible Nasdaq listing later—uncertain) ~43–44% electrical / ~90% CHP. Mature stationary technology; lower electrical efficiency than leading SOFC claims, strong when heat is valuable.

3.4 Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM)—and why PEM / H2 round-trip is usually wrong for baseload

Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) fuel cells (Plug Power NASDAQ: PLUG; Ballard Power NASDAQ: BLDP) use platinum and polymer membranes and require pure hydrogen. They fit vehicles, forklifts, and some backup niches—not multi-year AI baseload. Alkaline Fuel Cells (AFC) are cheaper but CO2-poisoned in ambient air without scrubbing.

Electrolyzing on-site (or grid) power to make hydrogen to make power again destroys most of the energy (see §4). Do not use PEM + electrolyzed H2 as a default AI hall baseload architecture.

3.5 Gas turbines, gensets, and hybrid

Large-frame turbines and fast-start gensets remain the workhorse for firm capacity and bridging interconnect delays:

  • Mitsubishi Heavy (TSE: 7011): M501 JAC large-frame gas turbines (capacity expansion plans cited through FY2030; $618M+ Japan/US investment context; ~74 unit backlog—figures as of baseline notes); MGS diesel gensets 0.5–3.4 MW with ~10 s start; 10 MW-class chillers; Turboden Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC) waste-heat recovery; DIAVAULT edge halls.
  • Hybrid: turbines/gensets for ramp and redundancy + SOFC (or other fuel cells) for steadier, higher-efficiency baseload DC. Hybrids hedge SOFC fade and turbine interconnect timing.

4. Hydrogen path math — when H2 makes sense (and when it does not)

Electrolysis typically consumes ~50–55 kWh of electricity per kilogram of hydrogen, while the kilogram itself stores ~33 kWh of chemical energy (lower heating value convention). Passing that H2 through a fuel cell at 40–60% electrical efficiency yields a round-trip often in the 25–35% band.

Do not do this for AI baseload

Do not electrolyze on-site (or grid) power to make hydrogen to make power again for continuous AI halls. You burn most of the energy twice. Use H2 only for stranded renewables, seasonal storage, ships/planes/trucks, or industry feedstock—not as a default wall-to-core path.

  • Global hydrogen is still ~61% Steam Methane Reforming (SMR) “grey” gas; low-emissions hydrogen remains under ~1% of supply (baseline share).
  • Existing natural-gas pipes are not 100% H2-ready (embrittlement). Dedicated H2 lines use lower-strength X42/X52 steel, lower pressure, ASME B31.12 practice, epoxy/polyurethane liners (not ceramics).
  • Transmission leak class ~0.04–0.24% per 1 000 km. Compression energy ~8% per 1 000 km class. Compare: HVAC ~5–8%/1 000 km; HVDC ~3.5%/1 000 km + ~1%/converter. Buried HVDC can cost ~10× overhead; pipe often beats overhead line on permitting, not on electron physics.

5. Site-boundary transmission and AC→DC conversion (SST vs legacy)

5.1 Legacy AC chain

Classic halls follow: grid AC → facility transformer → Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS) → Power Distribution Unit (PDU) (the breaker cabinet between UPS and racks) → per-server AC–DC Power Supply Unit (PSU) → 12 V → multiphase Voltage Regulators (VRs) / Voltage Regulator Modules (VRMs) to sub-1 V.

5.2 New AI path (~800 V) and what an SST is

New designs (Nvidia Rubin / 800 V class): medium-voltage AC → perimeter industrial rectifier or Solid-State Transformer (SST) to ~800 V DC (or ±400 V bipolar) → DC busway → rack/blade 800 V-to-12 V LLC converter (~64:1) → VRs.

An SST is not a 50/60 Hz copper-steel AC–AC transformer. It uses Silicon Carbide (SiC) switches plus a small high-frequency transformer. Commercial scale for data-center SST is roughly ~2027–2029. Jefferies has estimated the US data-center SST market could grow from tens of millions toward ~$4.3 B by 2030.

Typical hop efficiencies after the site boundary: SST/rectifier ~97%, 800 V bus ~99%, 800-to-12 V ~97.5%, VRMs ~90%.

6. In-hall DC distribution to GPU / CPU voltage regulators

Once power is on an ~800 V (or ±400 V) DC busway, distribution looks more like industrial DC than classic branch-circuit AC:

  1. DC busway / connectors — low-loss distribution within the hall (bus ~99% class). Connector and busway suppliers include Amphenol and peers.
  2. Rack or blade 800→12 V LLC — resonant conversion at ~64:1 ratio, ~97.5% class efficiency.
  3. Multiphase VRMs on the board or module — take 12 V (or intermediate rails) to core voltages below 1 V at ~90% class; heat density here is a first-order cooling problem.

Control and power silicon for these stages includes Texas Instruments, STMicroelectronics, Infineon, and Efficient Power Conversion (EPC) where Gallium Nitride (GaN) appears in some stages. Collapsing the legacy UPS/PDU/PSU cascade removes redundant conversion and transfers more of the thermal budget to useful compute.

5.3 How fast is the onsite AC → onsite DC (~800 V) transition?

Public hyperscaler / Open Compute Project (OCP) / vendor timelines (as of ~Oct 2026) describe a staged move—not a wholesale overnight replacement of facility Alternating Current (AC). The industry belief that new AI halls will land power on ~800 V Direct Current (DC) (or ±400 V bipolar) with no classic per-server AC Power Supply Unit (PSU) is directionally right for new high-density AI factories, but the path is phased.

Timeline synthesis (public sources)
  • 2025–mid 2026: NVIDIA, Google, and Microsoft drive OCP Low-Voltage Direct Current (LVDC) / 800 VDC work after March 2025 presentations; first LVDC white paper; MVAC→800 VDC conversion workstream; Solid-State Transformer (SST) spec v0.3 shared with the community (OCP blog, Aug 2026).
  • H2 2026 → 2027: Sidecar / side power rack path—convert existing 480 VAC locally to ±400 or 0–800 VDC beside the compute rack, preserving most upstream AC. Vertiv cites portfolio commercialization in H2 2026 and deployment ramp through 2027 (PowerDirect-class 400–900 kW sidecars). NVIDIA targets 800 V HVDC infrastructure alongside Kyber rack-scale systems starting ~2027.
  • ~2028–2029+: Facility-wide / data-hall direct Medium-Voltage Alternating Current (MVAC) → 800 VDC via MW-scale transformer-rectifiers or SST skids (OCP path B). This is when “no AC PSU in servers” becomes the default for purpose-built AI factories rather than a retrofit option.

How fast in practice? Expect material sidecar / rack-adjacent 800 VDC volume in new AI densification from ~2027, with facility-native 800 VDC halls scaling through ~2028–2030. Existing AC halls are not forklift-upgraded overnight—OCP explicitly frames 800 VDC as an additional deployment option that coexists with AC. “No AC PSU” applies first to 800 VDC-native racks (power conversion moves to sidecar or perimeter), not to every legacy server in the fleet.

Primary public references used for this synthesis: OCP LVDC / 800 VDC collaboration post (Google, Microsoft, NVIDIA; Aug 2026); NVIDIA 800 V HVDC architecture technical blog (Kyber ~2027 framing); Vertiv “practical path to 800 VDC” commercialization notes. Dates and product names can slip—treat as industry trajectory, not a procurement schedule.

7. Bottlenecks

Transformers (legacy LF)

Low-frequency copper-steel facility and grid transformers: lead times often cited 2–5 years. This is the headline shortage—not SST high-frequency magnetics.

SiC wafers / PVT / epitaxy

Silicon Carbide device supply hinges on Physical Vapor Transport (PVT) crystal-growth furnaces and epitaxy reactors. Aehr (AEHR) makes wafer-level burn-in testers that qualify SiC parts—it does not grow crystals.

Scandium (Bloom ScSZ)

Scandium oxide ~40–80 t/yr global; Bloom’s ScSZ path is comparatively exposed. Ceres and most other SOFC designs avoid scandium.

Interconnects & time-to-power

Utility interconnection queues and switchgear can dominate schedule. Fuel cells often win months; a month early on 1 GW AI can be ~$1–2 B compute context.

MV switchgear / substation gear

Engineered medium-voltage switchgear and specialty gear remain elevated lead-time items. With large power transformers, this is a binding schedule gate through ~2027 — red-framed stages on the diagrams.

800 VDC conversion gear (near-term watch)

Sidecar / LLC / busway / connector supply must scale with Kyber-class and OCP Diablo paths from ~2027. Yellow-framed dc-bus / llc / site-sst stages. SiC device fab deep-dives live under Semiconductor Fabrication — this page owns SST systems and power delivery.

Additional friction: HVDC converter-station cost/lead time for long offsite links; H2 embrittlement if someone forces a molecule path; platinum group metals for PEM (niche for AI baseload); skilled labor for MV SST commissioning as products scale ~2027–2029.

8. Power delivery, grid interconnection & utility regulation

Even when generation exists somewhere on the system, an AI campus still needs power delivery—transformers, switchgear, electrical distribution, and interconnection work that moves megawatts from the fence to the hall. Those buildout lead times are a binding capacity constraint through roughly ~2027 in many market narratives. Separately, regulated utilities earn under Public Utility Commission rate frameworks, which is why most of them do not trade like pure AI-themed names even when data-center load is the growth story. 8.1 Power delivery & grid interconnection (binding through ~2027)

Power delivery / grid interconnection, in plain English, is everything required to take utility-scale (or campus-scale) electricity and land it safely at the data-center boundary and inside the campus: medium- and high-voltage transformers, switchgear (the switching and protection cabinets that isolate faults), busways and distribution gear, plus the utility studies, upgrades, and construction schedule that complete an interconnect. Cooling gear that sits adjacent to the power path (especially liquid cooling for dense Graphics Processing Unit / GPU halls) often appears in the same buildout conversation because heat removal and electrical capacity scale together.

Why this binds capacity through ~2027 even when “generation exists”:

  • Generation ≠ deliverable megawatts at a site. A Combined-Cycle Gas Turbine (CCGT) or renewable plant elsewhere still leaves a campus waiting on transformers, switchgear, and interconnection queue time at that location.
  • Legacy copper-steel transformers (50/60 Hz facility and grid units) carry multi-year lead times—often cited in the 2–5 year class on this page’s bottlenecks. That is a different product class from high-frequency Solid-State Transformers (SSTs), which are still scaling commercially toward the late 2020s.
  • Switchgear, skilled labor, and utility process sit on the critical path: studies, substation upgrades, and protection coordination cannot be skipped because silicon is ready.

8.2 Utility regulation — why most utilities aren’t surging like pure AI names

Most U.S. electric utilities do not freely set customer prices. Public utility commissions (state regulators) approve rates in rate cases, aiming for a roughly ~9–10% return on equity (ROE)—the allowed profit rate on the equity portion of the rate base. Commissions often haircut what utilities request: lower allowed ROE, disallowed capital, or both. That regulatory ceiling is the main reason regulated utilities usually do not re-rate like unconstrained “AI pure-play” equities even when data-center load is rising.

2025 rate-case context (Oct 2026 briefing notes)

Industry profits around $63.1 B, but about $3.6 B cut from rate requests (~5× the 2021 haircut level). Disallowances in roughly ~46% of cases; ROE haircuts often cited in the 78–85 basis points range (a basis point is 0.01 percentage points). Political backdrop: 36 state governor races in November 2026 keep rate politics salient. Treat as briefing-note context, not audited totals.

Three common structures (same allowed-ROE logic for wires, different exposure to wholesale power prices for generation):

  • Merchant generators (examples often named: Constellation, Vistra, Talen) — sell power at wholesale market prices or under Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs). Earnings move with power prices and offtake contracts rather than a fixed rate-base ROE. Briefing-note factoid: Constellation’s share price roughly tripled from 2022 lows (historical price move only—not a recommendation).
  • Integrated utilities (examples: American Electric Power (AEP), Entergy, Duke Energy, Southern Company) — own generation and wires; earn primarily on regulated rate base as they invest. Company / market-narrative examples from the notes (guidance and framing, not advice): AEP ~69 GW by 2030 (~90% data-center-related in that framing), ~$78 B capital expenditure, ~11% rate-base compound annual growth rate (CAGR); Entergy ~13% earnings-per-share (EPS) CAGR through 2030 in company guidance narratives.
  • Wires-only utilities (examples: National Grid, Eversource, Exelon, Consolidated Edison (ConEd)) — transmission and distribution under the same ROE framework without merchant generation upside. Narratives in the notes: Eversource resisting some large data-center loads; Exelon’s interconnection pipeline described as shrinking from roughly 18→11 GW.

Beneficiary-pays. More than 25 utilities across 19 U.S. states have filed data-center-specific or beneficiary-pays tariffs: the large load that drives upgrades should cover related network costs so ordinary retail ratepayers are not stuck with the bill. The White House Ratepayer Protection Pledge (March 2026) asks hyperscalers to build, bring, or buy generation (and related infrastructure) toward that same idea. Demand-side detail: Hyperscalers §5.

Large planned buildout example. NextEra–Dominion package framed around roughly $66.8 B for “Data Center Alley” load on the order of ~51 GW—an illustration of utility-scale partnership size under regulation, not a forecast or recommendation.

Glossary

AC / DC
Alternating Current / Direct Current.
AFC
Alkaline Fuel Cell — cheap but CO2-poisoned without scrubbing.
CCGT
Combined-Cycle Gas Turbine — gas turbine plus steam bottoming cycle; ~60–64% LHV electrical.
CHP
Combined Heat and Power — electrical plus useful thermal output.
CPU / GPU
Central Processing Unit / Graphics Processing Unit.
GaN
Gallium Nitride — wide-bandgap semiconductor used in some converter stages.
HVAC / HVDC
High-Voltage Alternating Current / High-Voltage Direct Current transmission.
LHV
Lower Heating Value — heating-value basis that excludes condensation heat of water.
LLC
Resonant converter topology (inductor-inductor-capacitor) used in 800→12 V stages.
MCFC
Molten Carbonate Fuel Cell.
MV
Medium Voltage — distribution class voltages into the facility perimeter.
ORC
Organic Rankine Cycle — waste-heat-to-power using an organic working fluid.
PAFC
Phosphoric Acid Fuel Cell.
PDU
Power Distribution Unit — breaker cabinet between UPS and racks.
PEM
Proton Exchange Membrane (fuel cell or electrolyzer context).
PSU
Power Supply Unit — per-server AC–DC converter in legacy designs.
PVT
Physical Vapor Transport — SiC crystal-growth method; furnace-limited.
ScSZ / YSZ
Scandium-Stabilized Zirconia / Yttria-Stabilized Zirconia — SOFC electrolyte ceramics.
SiC
Silicon Carbide — wide-bandgap switches enabling SSTs and efficient MV conversion.
SMR
Steam Methane Reforming — dominant grey hydrogen route (~61% of global H2).
SOFC
Solid Oxide Fuel Cell — high-temperature ceramic fuel cell; can reform NG to DC.
SST
Solid-State Transformer — SiC switches + high-frequency transformer; not a 50/60 Hz Cu-Fe unit.
UPS
Uninterruptible Power Supply.
VR / VRM
Voltage Regulator / Voltage Regulator Module — final multiphase conversion to sub-1 V.
