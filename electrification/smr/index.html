SMR — Advanced Nuclear

Why nuclear shows up in the AI power story, how merchant plants and offtake contracts fit, and weekly scorecards for public reactor developers.

As of Oct 1, 2026

Emerging theme — nuclear for AI load

Near-term AI buildouts often hit nearer bottlenecks first: power equipment, packaging, and high-bandwidth memory (HBM). After those, the conversation often turns to 24/7 clean electricity — plants that run day and night without depending on weather. That is where nuclear power and small modular reactors (SMRs) enter the picture.

An SMR is a nuclear reactor built in factory-sized modules instead of one giant custom plant. The idea is shorter build times and the ability to add capacity in steps. Most SMRs are still in licensing or early construction; many AI campuses that need power this decade also look at existing large reactors (restarts, life extensions) and long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) — contracts to buy electricity at agreed terms.

Merchant nuclear vs regulated utilities

A regulated utility usually earns a fixed allowed return set by state regulators. When power is scarce, that structure often does not let shareholders keep “scarcity rents” the way a competitive market plant can.

Merchant nuclear owners sell into wholesale markets or under private contracts. When AI and cloud companies (often called hyperscalers) sign long PPAs, that path can be cleaner for investors who want exposure to contracted offtake rather than regulated rate cases alone.

Constellation Energy (CEG) is a useful example of the existing fleet + contracts story: large U.S. nuclear capacity, plus deals that restart or keep plants online for data-center and clean-power buyers (for example Microsoft’s Crane / Three Mile Island Unit 1 path). That thesis is more about AI-driven power availability than about being a pure SMR equipment maker. Public commentary has often noted a large re-rating from 2022 lows as AI power demand rose — treat price history as context, not a forecast.

Utility combinations that add generation optionality near dense data-center corridors (sometimes called “Data Center Alley” in Northern Virginia) also matter for the same reason: more controlled supply next to load. The NextEra–Dominion combination (~$66.8B in the brief’s framing) is one illustration of generation optionality aimed at that corridor (~51 GW of regional demand scale in the same brief). Details change with filings — check primary sources before relying on any one number.

Ratepayer Protection Pledge (March 2026)

In March 2026 the White House published a voluntary Ratepayer Protection Pledge. In plain terms, large AI and cloud companies agree to build, bring, or buy the generation their data centers need, and to pay for related delivery upgrades, so ordinary household bills are not the first place AI load shows up.

That framing is supportive for nuclear and SMR offtake: if hyperscalers must bring new or additive supply, long nuclear PPAs and SMR campus deals become a natural matching product — alongside gas, renewables, and storage. The pledge is voluntary policy signaling, not a guarantee that any one project gets built.
